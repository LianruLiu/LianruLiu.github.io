## Introduction  
In many classrooms, ranking isn't just a score summary — it's a visible social hierarchy.
Who ranks first often becomes a symbol:  
- Teachers mention them  
- Parents compare children to them  
- Students use them as benchmarks  
- Expectations shift around them  

This raises a deeper question:  
> If the number one student in class is a girl, does it change how other girls perform?

A large-scale study using Chinese junior-high data gives a serious answer:

> Yes — and the effect is economically meaningful.

---

## I. The Research Question  
The paper studies whether the **gender of the top-ranked student in class** affects classmates’ later academic outcomes.  
It sounds trivial at first.  
But in tournament-style education systems — East Asia especially — rankings are brutally visible. “First place” carries symbolic weight far beyond a test score.  
The researchers ask:  
- Does a female top scorer become a role model for other girls?  
- Does it raise confidence and ambition?  
- Does it alter parental expectations?  
- Or does it make no difference at all?

---
## II. Method  
The study uses nationally representative Chinese junior-high data, focusing on classes where:  
- The highest-scoring girl and highest-scoring boy had nearly identical scores  
- Sometimes the girl barely ranked first  
- Sometimes the boy barely ranked first  

This creates a quasi-random comparison.  
Instead of comparing wildly different classrooms, they compare classes where the only real difference is:  
> Whether the first-ranked student was female or male.

The method is called **regression discontinuity design (RDD)** — a standard tool for causal inference.

---
## III. Results  
The findings are striking.  
When the top-ranked student was a girl:  
### Other girls in the class later experienced:  
- Roughly **0.4 standard deviations** of improvement in next-year total scores  
- Better within-grade rankings  
- Gains across Chinese, Math, and English  
- Strongest effects among disadvantaged girls  

### Boys in the same class:  
- Showed little or no statistically significant change

In short:  
> A female number one student substantially lifts other girls, while boys remain mostly unaffected.

---
## IV. Why Does This Happen?  
The paper’s answer: it’s not better teachers or easier classes.  
It’s primarily a **role model effect**.  
When girls see another girl become number one, several things change.

### 1. Self-Belief Rises  
Girls start believing more in their own academic potential.  
Instead of thinking:  
- “Top students are usually boys”

They begin thinking:  
- “Someone like me can be first.”

### 2. Aspirations Increase  
They aim higher and compete harder.  
### 3. Study Effort Improves    
More focus, more commitment, better use of time.  
### 4. Parents React Too  
Parents of daughters raise their expectations and invest more when they see a girl succeeding visibly.

---
## V. Who Gains Most  
The most important finding:  
The biggest gains came from girls who were:  
- In the lower half of the class ranking  
- From rural backgrounds  
- From less educated families  

Elite students usually have confidence and support already.  
But for girls with fewer advantages:  
> Seeing a female classmate rank first may fundamentally reshape what feels possible.

---
## VI. Why This Matters Beyond School  
The broader point:  
> Representation changes behavior when success is visible.

The same mechanism appears in many domains:  
- Female CEOs influencing young women’s career ambitions  
- Female professors increasing female participation in STEM  
- Female political leaders changing girls’ aspirations  
- Successful peers expanding perceived opportunity sets  

People often need evidence before belief.  
Visible success is evidence.

---
## VII. My View  
People usually blame underperformance on ability.  
Often it comes from:  
- Low expectations  
- Weak confidence  
- No relatable examples  
- Internalized stereotypes  

This study suggests one visible achiever can loosen those constraints.  
Especially for girls in competitive systems:  
> The first-ranked female student is not just winning for herself.  
> She changes the reference point for everyone behind her.

---
## VIII. Conclusion  
Does a female number one student improve other girls’ performance?  
According to rigorous evidence from Chinese junior high schools:

> Yes — substantially.

Not through slogans.  
Not through policy campaigns.  
Not through motivational speeches.

But through something simpler:

> Seeing someone similar succeed at the highest visible level.

Sometimes progress begins when one person stands at the top — and others realize they can stand there too!
