On May 13, 2026, the U.S. Senate confirmed Kevin Warsh as the 11th chair of the Federal Reserve in the narrowest confirmation vote in the central bank's modern history — 54 to 45, almost entirely along party lines. Warsh, 56, takes the helm at a moment of unusual fiscal and geopolitical stress: a national debt approaching $39 trillion, annual interest payments that have surpassed national defense spending, an ongoing conflict in the Middle East driving energy inflation, and a president openly demanding rate cuts.

Understanding what Warsh is likely to do — and why the arithmetic of U.S. public finance constrains the policy options far more than political preferences — requires starting with the debt itself.

---

## Part I: The Fiscal Reality — When Interest Becomes the Budget

### A Trillion-Dollar Line Item

The United States spent approximately $970 billion on net interest payments on its national debt in fiscal year 2025. That number exceeded what the government spent on national defense ($917 billion), Medicaid ($668 billion), veterans' benefits, transportation, education, and every other discretionary program — combined. Only Social Security commanded a larger share of the budget.

For fiscal year 2026, the Congressional Budget Office projects interest costs will cross $1 trillion for the first time, representing roughly 19 cents of every dollar of federal revenue collected. For every dollar of taxes and fees the government collects, 19 cents goes directly to paying interest on past debt — before a single dollar is spent on defense, healthcare, infrastructure, or anything else.

This is not a projection about a distant future. Interest payments in the first quarter of FY 2026 (October through December 2025) alone totaled $270 billion — already exceeding the entire interest bill for all of fiscal year 2017 ($263 billion). The trajectory is not ambiguous.

Looking further out, CBO estimates that interest payments will grow by 106 percent over the 2026–2036 budget window, rising from $1.0 trillion to $2.1 trillion annually. By 2048, under current projections, interest will become the single largest item in the federal budget — surpassing even Social Security.

This is the fiscal context in which Kevin Warsh assumes the chairmanship. It is not a neutral backdrop. It actively constrains the available policy space in ways that would not exist if the debt level were lower or the interest rate environment were more forgiving.

### The Maturity Structure Problem: Short-Term Debt and Rollover Risk

The composition of who holds U.S. Treasury debt matters as much as the aggregate level, and here the picture is more nuanced than the headline numbers suggest.

As of December 2025, total U.S. publicly held federal debt stood at approximately $30.1 trillion, with foreign investors holding $9.2 trillion — roughly 31% of the total. The top three foreign holders are Japan ($1.19 trillion), the United Kingdom ($866 billion), and China ($684 billion). EU member states collectively hold an additional substantial position in both Treasuries and U.S. equities.

The debt stock can be usefully divided by maturity and holder type:

**Short-term Treasuries (T-bills, under one year):** These are primarily held by money market funds, corporate treasuries, commercial banks, and foreign investors who treat them as dollar liquidity rather than as long-duration investments. Short-term debt is cheap to issue when rates are low but extraordinarily sensitive to rate changes — and must be continuously rolled over. At current debt levels, even a 100 basis point rise in average short-term rates translates into roughly $300 billion in additional annual interest expense.

**Medium- and long-term Treasuries (2 to 30 years):** These are the preferred holdings of insurance companies, pension funds, and sovereign wealth funds — institutions with long-duration liability structures that match the cash flow profile of longer-dated bonds. These holders are less sensitive to short-term rate volatility but are highly sensitive to credit risk perceptions and long-term inflation expectations.

**Foreign sovereign and institutional holders:** Japan and major European creditors hold significant positions — much of it in longer-dated Treasuries. A critical dimension of this foreign holding structure is the role of **currency swap arrangements**. Foreign central banks and sovereign investors often hedge their dollar exposure through FX swap markets: borrowing dollars to purchase Treasuries, while simultaneously entering forward contracts to sell those dollars back at a set future rate in exchange for yen, euros, or other currencies. The dollar proceeds from hedging swaps are frequently recycled into global equity markets, including U.S. equities. This creates a mechanism by which U.S. Treasury issuance — particularly at the short end — indirectly supports global equity market liquidity.

The benefit of this arrangement is that it sustains robust demand for U.S. short-term debt even at lower yields, and provides a structural bid for risk assets. The risk is that it creates fragility: when dollar funding costs rise sharply (as they did in March 2020 and again during the 2022 rate shock), the unwinding of swap-funded Treasury positions can generate sudden, violent dislocations in both bond and equity markets. The swap basis — the cost of borrowing dollars synthetically versus directly — becomes a real-time indicator of global dollar funding stress.

## Part II: The Rate Environment — Why Rate Hikes Are Off the Table

### The Arithmetic of Hikes at This Debt Level

The most important thing to understand about the current interest rate debate is that the traditional inflation-fighting toolkit — raising rates aggressively until inflation breaks — operates very differently at a debt-to-GDP ratio approaching 100% versus the 40–60% levels that characterized most of the post-WWII era.

When the debt is $39 trillion, each 100 basis point increase in the average rate on outstanding debt eventually translates into approximately $390 billion of additional annual interest expense. Given that current interest payments are already $1 trillion per year and represent 19% of total revenue, the fiscal headroom for sustained rate increases is extremely limited. Hikes that would have been manageable at 2015 debt levels become structurally dangerous at 2026 levels — not because the rate level itself is fatal, but because the combination of rate, debt stock, and deficit trajectory creates a self-reinforcing loop: higher rates → higher deficits → more issuance → upward pressure on yields → higher rates.

This dynamic is precisely what some analysts call a "fiscal dominance" scenario — a condition in which the debt situation constrains monetary policy, rather than monetary policy operating freely. The U.S. is not in full fiscal dominance today. But at $39 trillion of gross debt, the corridor between "normal monetary policy" and "fiscal constraint binding" is narrower than at any point since World War II.

The data supports this reading: at the FOMC's April 2026 meeting — with inflation still running at 3.8% annually, well above the 2% target — the committee nonetheless left rates unchanged for the third meeting in a row. The Iran conflict provided a political cover for the hold, but the fiscal arithmetic was present regardless of geopolitical noise.

### Rate Hikes in 2026: Low Probability, High Cost

With inflation running at 3.8% and above the 2% target, rate hikes are technically justifiable on price stability grounds. But the cost-benefit calculus runs strongly against them in the current fiscal environment. Hiking would:

- Raise interest costs on the $39 trillion debt stack at a time when the government is already allocating 19 cents of every revenue dollar to interest
- Compress bank net interest margins if short rates rise faster than long rates (flattening or inverting the curve)
- Suppress equity market valuations through higher discount rates — which has a direct fiscal feedback effect given that capital gains taxes represent a meaningful and growing component of federal revenue
- Potentially disrupt the swap-funded Treasury demand from foreign holders, at exactly the moment when the Treasury needs maximum demand absorption

Three members of the FOMC, according to minutes from the April 2026 meeting, suggested the next move could as easily be a hike as a cut. This is a minority position. The fiscal reality — and Warsh's stated preference for a "smaller imprint" for the Fed in the bond market — points strongly in the opposite direction.

## Part III: The Warsh Playbook — Rate Cuts and Quantitative Tightening Together

### An Unusual Combination

Warsh's monetary policy framework, as articulated in his Senate testimony and prior public statements, points toward a combination that markets have not previously encountered in quite this configuration: **cutting the policy rate while simultaneously reducing the Fed's balance sheet** — rate cuts with continued quantitative tightening (QT).

On the surface, this seems contradictory. Rate cuts are typically stimulative; QT is typically restrictive. But the logic is coherent when viewed through the lens of the current fiscal and financial structure.

### Why Rate Cuts Make Sense Now

The federal funds rate currently sits in a range that has held for three consecutive meetings. Warsh has argued that there is room to ease — and the structural reasons are compelling:

**Debt service cost reduction.** Short-term Treasury rates are closely tied to the federal funds rate. As the Treasury has shifted toward shorter-duration issuance in recent years (to minimize rollover cost in a high-rate environment), a substantial portion of the outstanding debt stock reprices relatively quickly when the Fed cuts. Each 25 basis point cut, compounding over the short-term rollover cycle, meaningfully reduces the government's annual interest bill.

**Yield curve normalization.** An extended period of rate cuts would re-steepen the yield curve — making longer-duration Treasuries relatively more attractive to insurance companies, pension funds, and other long-duration investors. This is important: encouraging natural migration of Treasury holdings from the short end (held by money markets and foreign swap traders) to the long end (held by structural investors with matched liabilities) improves the stability of the funding base.

**Equity market support.** Lower short-term rates reduce the discount rate applied to future earnings, supporting equity valuations. Given that capital gains and corporate income taxes represent a structurally important and increasingly large component of federal revenue — particularly in the current AI investment cycle, where technology sector valuations have driven outsized capital gains realizations — a healthier equity market directly supports fiscal revenues.

**Easing pressure on commercial banks.** Commercial banks hold significant quantities of both short-term Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities on their balance sheets. Rate cuts, combined with yield curve steepening, improve bank net interest margins and reduce mark-to-market losses on their fixed-income portfolios. This matters for monetary transmission: banks that are under less balance sheet stress are more willing to extend credit to the real economy.

### Why QT (Balance Sheet Reduction) Continues

Here is where Warsh diverges from a simple "dovish pivot" narrative. He has been a long-standing advocate for reducing the Fed's balance sheet — which currently stands at approximately $6.7 trillion. His argument is structural rather than cyclical: the Fed accumulated Treasury and mortgage-backed securities through multiple rounds of quantitative easing that went well beyond crisis management, and an oversized balance sheet distorts asset pricing and creates artificial suppression of term premia.

The proposed mechanism under a Warsh framework is one where:

- The Fed shifts its balance sheet composition **toward shorter-duration Treasuries** as longer-dated holdings mature, rather than reinvesting in new long-duration securities. This reduces the Fed's footprint in the long end of the market without a disruptive outright sale program.
- Simultaneously, **commercial banks are encouraged** — through regulatory signals or capital framework adjustments — to absorb more medium- and long-term Treasury issuance. Banks can serve as natural buyers of the duration that the Fed is releasing, particularly as a steeper yield curve makes longer-dated assets more profitable to hold.
- This creates a managed handoff: Fed-held long-duration Treasuries are replaced by bank-held long-duration Treasuries, while the Fed retains or expands its short-term Treasury holdings to maintain liquidity management capacity.

The operational logic: by managing its short-term holdings actively, the Fed preserves its influence over short-term rates (the primary transmission mechanism of monetary policy) while reducing its distorting presence in the long-duration market. Banks, with their deposit-funded balance sheets and interest rate risk management capacity, are better suited as long-term holders of Treasuries than a central bank whose mandate is price stability rather than asset management.

The potential Fed-Treasury accord that Warsh has referenced — though the details remain vague — may formalize some version of this arrangement: a coordination framework in which the Treasury's issuance decisions (maturity profile, volume timing) and the Fed's balance sheet management are aligned to reduce market disruption without compromising monetary independence.

## Part IV: The Equity Market Dimension

### The Circular Relationship Between Wall Street and Washington

There is a channel in the U.S. fiscal system that rarely features in mainstream budget discussions but has grown substantially in importance: the relationship between equity market performance and federal tax revenue.

As the stock market has grown relative to GDP — driven by decades of earnings growth, multiple expansion, and more recently the AI investment cycle — capital gains realizations and corporate tax payments tied to financial sector profitability have become a larger and more volatile component of federal revenue. A bull market in equities is, in a literal sense, a revenue tailwind for the federal government.

This creates a feedback loop: the government has a fiscal interest in not undermining equity valuations through unnecessarily restrictive monetary policy; rate cuts support valuations; stronger valuations generate more capital gains realizations; higher capital gains realizations reduce the deficit at the margin.

The channel runs in both directions, of course. A sharp equity market decline — driven by, for example, an unexpected rate hike or a credit event — reduces capital gains revenue at the worst possible time, widening the deficit precisely when the government's fiscal position is most stressed.

### Foreign Capital and the Swap-Equity Nexus

The foreign holder dimension adds another layer. Major foreign creditors — Japan most prominently — hold large quantities of U.S. Treasuries and use swap markets to hedge their dollar exposure. The mechanics of these hedges involve borrowing yen cheaply (given the Bank of Japan's historically low policy rate) and converting to dollars, which are then deployed into U.S. assets including both Treasuries and equities.

When the Fed cuts rates, it narrows the interest rate differential between dollars and yen, making the yen carry trade less attractive and potentially reducing the flow of foreign capital into U.S. markets. This is a genuine risk to the rate-cut thesis — and it partly explains why Warsh is unlikely to cut aggressively in the early phase of his tenure, even if the direction of travel is toward lower rates.

The balance point: sufficient rate reduction to ease the government's interest burden and support equity market valuations, but not so aggressive as to invert the incentive structure that sustains foreign demand for dollar-denominated assets.

## Part V: A Framework Summary

The picture that emerges from this analysis is not a simple "Warsh will cut rates because Trump wants him to" narrative. It is considerably more structured:

| Dimension | Current Situation | Warsh Likely Direction | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Policy rate | Held for 3 meetings | Gradual cuts, 25bp increments | Debt service relief; yield curve normalization |
| Balance sheet | ~$6.7T, QT ongoing | Continue QT, shift to shorter duration | Reduce market distortion; preserve liquidity tools |
| Long-end Treasury demand | Fed-held, rolling off | Bank absorption encouraged | Handoff of duration risk to structural holders |
| Short-end liquidity | Fed active | Maintain capacity | Control short rates as primary policy lever |
| Equity market | High but sensitive to Iran conflict | Supported indirectly via rate path | Revenue implications; financial conditions |
| Foreign holder dynamics | $9.2T, swap-funded | Monitor carefully | Dollar demand; carry trade sensitivity |
| Rate hikes | Market pricing small probability | Very low likelihood | Fiscal arithmetic; regulatory cost |

The core of the Warsh framework, as it can be currently reconstructed from his public statements and the structural constraints he inherits, is: **ease the price of money modestly while reducing the quantity of central bank balance sheet intervention**. It is a combination designed to normalize monetary policy without triggering a fiscal crisis — threading a needle between an inflation target that has not been met and a debt stock that punishes rate increases severely.

## The Risk Factors

No analysis of this framework would be complete without acknowledging what could go wrong.

**Inflation persistence.** At 3.8% annual CPI, with energy prices elevated by the Iran conflict, the inflation mandate is not being met. If Warsh cuts rates while inflation remains above target, he risks repeating the policy error that the Fed made in 2021 — being "behind the curve" in the direction of loose policy rather than tight. His hawkish reputation from his 2006–2011 tenure would be tested immediately.

**Bond market discipline.** If bond markets conclude that the Fed-Treasury relationship is being subordinated to fiscal convenience, long-term yields could rise independently of Fed policy — a steepening driven by inflation risk premium rather than economic recovery. This would be the worst possible outcome: a fiscal dominance scenario in which the government's interest burden rises despite the Fed's intentions.

**Geopolitical disruption.** The ongoing Middle East conflict has already affected oil prices and inflation. Further escalation could force a reassessment of the entire rate path in either direction.

**Execution risk on balance sheet management.** The proposed handoff of long-duration Treasuries from the Fed to commercial banks requires banks to have both the capital and the appetite to absorb the supply. If neither condition holds, the release of duration from the Fed's portfolio could push long-term yields higher, tightening financial conditions even as short rates fall.

---

*The United States is attempting to manage a $39 trillion debt stack, a trillion-dollar annual interest bill, and an inflation problem simultaneously — with a new central banker whose political positioning is complex and whose operational framework is still being fully disclosed. The outcome will be consequential not just for American markets, but for every institution globally that holds dollars, dollar-denominated debt, or dollar-denominated equity as a reserve asset.*

*The rate cycle is turning. The balance sheet is shrinking. The equity market is watching both. And the fiscal clock is running.*
