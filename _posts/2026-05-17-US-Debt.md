Kevin Warsh was confirmed as Chair of the Federal Reserve on May 13, 2026, in a 54–45 vote — the most partisan confirmation in Fed history. He takes the helm of an institution under extraordinary pressure, inheriting an economy burdened by structural fiscal dysfunction, elevated inflation complicated by a Middle East energy shock, and a Treasury market whose plumbing is quietly becoming more fragile. Understanding what Warsh is likely to do — and why — requires first understanding the fiscal trap that constrains every option available to him.

---

## Part I: The Fiscal Problem — Debt Is Now the Dominant Budget Item

The numbers are no longer abstract. As of early 2026, total U.S. public debt stands at approximately $38.6 trillion. Interest payments on that debt hit a new record in 2025, rising well above $1 trillion annually. In Q4 2025 alone, interest payments reached $307 billion — consuming 31.6% of quarterly tax receipts. Debt service has become the single largest and fastest-growing category in the federal budget, crowding out everything else.

The Congressional Budget Office projects debt held by the public rising from 101% of GDP in 2026 to 120% by 2036 — and to 175% by 2056 if current trajectories hold, even accounting for revenue from new tariff regimes. These are not tail-risk scenarios. They are the baseline.

The structural arithmetic is straightforward: the federal government is running a deficit of approximately 6% of GDP annually — roughly double the historical norm. That deficit requires continuous issuance of new Treasury securities to finance, which feeds a debt stock that compounds interest payments, which widens the deficit further. The feedback loop is not self-correcting under current policy.

This is the fiscal backdrop against which every monetary policy decision is made. It fundamentally constrains the Fed's room to maneuver — and it shapes what "independent" monetary policy can actually mean in practice.

## Part II: Who Holds the Debt — and Why It Matters

The Treasury market's stability depends entirely on the willingness of a diverse set of holders to absorb continuous issuance. Understanding who holds what — and why their behavior might change — is essential to understanding the fragility embedded in the current fiscal trajectory.

### Short-Term Treasury Bills: Corporations, Banks, and Money Market Funds

Treasury bills (maturities up to one year) are primarily held by domestic commercial banks, corporations managing cash, and money market funds. Their demand for T-bills is driven by yield relative to alternatives — particularly the Fed's policy rate. When the Fed cuts short-term rates, T-bill yields fall, making them less attractive relative to other short-duration instruments.

This creates a direct linkage: Fed rate cuts reduce the government's interest expense on its $6 trillion in outstanding T-bills, providing near-term fiscal relief. But they also potentially reduce demand from yield-sensitive holders if alternatives become more attractive. The balance is delicate.

### Foreign Holders: The Currency Swap Dimension

As of December 2025, foreign entities hold approximately $9.2 trillion in U.S. Treasury securities — 31% of publicly held debt. Japan remains the largest single foreign holder at $1.24 trillion, followed by the United Kingdom at $897 billion and China at $693 billion.

The Japan relationship deserves particular attention. Japanese institutional investors — banks, insurance companies, pension funds — are among the most significant marginal buyers of U.S. Treasuries. Their behavior is closely linked to the USD/JPY exchange rate and the Bank of Japan's policy trajectory. When the yen weakens substantially, Japanese holders face currency losses on their U.S. dollar assets; when it strengthens, the opposite. Currency swap agreements between the Fed and Bank of Japan exist precisely to manage the dollar liquidity implications of this interconnection.

Critically, foreign holders' Treasury positions and their equity market exposures are not independent. Japanese and European institutional investors often hold both U.S. Treasuries and U.S. equities as part of integrated portfolio strategies. A sharp Treasury market dislocation that forces foreign holders to reduce positions creates correlated selling pressure across both asset classes — a reminder that the bond and equity markets are not as separate as they often appear in analysis. The interconnection runs both directions.

### Medium- and Long-Term Bonds: Insurance Companies, Pension Funds, and Structural Erosion

Ten-year Treasury notes and 30-year bonds have historically been the natural domain of insurance companies and pension funds — institutions with long-dated liabilities that benefit from matching long-duration assets. Their demand for long Treasuries is structural, driven by asset-liability management rather than pure return-seeking.

But this buyer base faces a demographic problem. As the U.S. population ages, pension obligations come due and insurance claims increase. The aggregate asset pool managed by these institutions grows more slowly — and in some cases shrinks — as payouts exceed new contributions. The structural buyer base for long-duration Treasuries is gradually contracting relative to the supply of new issuance. This is a slow-moving but real deterioration in the demand side of the long-bond market.

### Hedge Funds and the Liquidity Backstop

With the traditional long-bond buyer base under demographic pressure, hedge funds have become an increasingly important source of liquidity in the intermediate and long Treasury market. Through basis trades, relative value strategies, and outright directional positions, leveraged money has taken on a larger role in absorbing Treasury supply and providing market-making capacity.

This matters because hedge fund participation is inherently more conditional than insurance company demand. A hedge fund will absorb Treasury supply when the risk/return is attractive; it will withdraw — rapidly — when it is not. During periods of market stress, when natural demand from real-money buyers is most needed, leveraged buyers are often simultaneously reducing risk. The liquidity provision that hedge funds provide in normal markets can become liquidity withdrawal in stressed ones. The March 2020 Treasury market dysfunction, when even benchmark Treasuries became difficult to trade at reasonable prices, illustrated this dynamic vividly.

## Part III: Why Rate Hikes Are Off the Table

Given inflation above target and an energy price shock from the Iran war, one might ask whether the Fed should be raising rates rather than preparing to cut them. The argument for hikes is straightforward in isolation: inflation is running above the 2% target, and tightening would reduce demand pressure.

The argument collapses when fiscal arithmetic enters the picture.

The U.S. government refinances an enormous volume of existing debt continuously. As Treasuries mature and are rolled over at current market rates, the average interest rate on the debt ratchets upward. Every 100 basis point increase in the average rate on $38 trillion of debt adds approximately $380 billion annually to interest expense — the equivalent of an entire discretionary spending category. At current rate levels, the debt servicing burden is already consuming nearly a third of tax receipts. A significant rate hike would accelerate the compounding of this burden in a way that would require either dramatic spending cuts (politically implausible), substantial tax increases (similarly constrained), or monetization (inflationary — the opposite of the stated goal).

There is also the equity market channel, which feeds directly into fiscal revenues. Capital gains taxes are one of the most volatile and economically significant components of federal tax receipts. A growing economy and rising asset prices generate taxable realization events; a market selloff driven by aggressive rate hikes generates the opposite. The U.S. government is not merely a passive observer of equity market performance — it is a direct fiscal beneficiary of it. A sharp equity decline, in addition to its economic demand effects, would reduce capital gains tax revenues precisely when deficit pressures are most acute.

This does not mean hikes are literally impossible — if inflation becomes severely entrenched and the Middle East situation worsens materially, the FOMC could be forced into them. But under any scenario that resembles the current one, rate hikes are a lever the fiscal situation cannot afford to pull at scale. CME FedWatch currently prices a 97% probability of no change at Warsh's first meeting in June, with the base case being stable rates for the remainder of 2026.

## Part IV: The Warsh Framework — Rate Cuts Plus Balance Sheet Reduction

Warsh has articulated a distinctive and internally coherent monetary policy framework that differs from conventional thinking on the relationship between rate cuts and balance sheet policy.

The conventional view treats rate cuts and balance sheet expansion (quantitative easing) as complementary tools — when you want to ease financial conditions, you do both. Warsh's view inverts this: he argues that a smaller balance sheet and a lower policy rate can coexist, and indeed that the former enables the latter.

His logic: the Fed's large balance sheet has distorted the long end of the yield curve, suppressed term premium, and created financial conditions that are artificially loose at the long end even when the policy rate is restrictive at the short end. By reducing the balance sheet — allowing longer-duration assets to roll off without replacement — the Fed normalizes the long end, restores term premium, and re-anchors the yield curve to reflect actual risk. This creates the conditions under which a lower policy rate is sustainable without generating inflationary financial conditions.

He has stated this framing explicitly: "In a way, one can afford a lower interest rate with a smaller balance sheet." The implication is that QT (quantitative tightening) and rate cuts are not contradictory — they are complementary tools aimed at different parts of the yield curve.

In operational terms, the likely playbook involves:

**Increasing the Fed's short-term bill holdings.** The Fed has already begun buying short-term Treasury bills to maintain adequate banking system reserves. Expanding this — allowing the Fed to hold more short-duration instruments while reducing long-duration holdings — reduces the Fed's footprint in the long-bond market without creating a reserve shortage that would destabilize short-term funding. This lowers the systemic liquidity risk of balance sheet reduction.

**Encouraging commercial banks to absorb more medium-duration Treasuries.** As the Fed reduces its long-bond holdings, the natural purchaser to replace that demand is the commercial banking sector. Banks, which hold substantial reserves that currently earn the interest-on-reserve rate, have an economic incentive to redeploy those reserves into higher-yielding Treasuries when the rate differential is attractive. Regulatory adjustments — particularly to the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) — could increase the capacity of banks to hold Treasuries without binding their capital constraints.

**Cutting the short-term policy rate.** As the balance sheet normalizes and long rates are reset to reflect genuine risk, there is more room to reduce the policy rate without re-igniting inflation. Lower short rates reduce the government's borrowing costs on T-bills, provide some relief to rate-sensitive sectors of the economy, and — critically — support equity market valuations through the standard discounting mechanism.

Warsh has acknowledged the constraints clearly: balance sheet reduction "isn't something that can or should happen quickly," requires FOMC consensus rather than unilateral chair action, and will "take time, patience, and care." Any actual change needs a committee vote, and the FOMC is currently the most divided it has been since 1992, with four dissenters at the April meeting.

## Part V: The Equity Market as Fiscal Infrastructure

The connection between equity markets and federal fiscal health is understated in most public discourse about U.S. debt sustainability.

Capital gains taxes — generated when investors sell appreciated assets — are among the most responsive components of federal revenue to market performance. In years when equities deliver strong returns and investors realize gains, capital gains tax receipts surge. In years of market declines, they collapse. This asymmetry means that equity market performance is not merely an economic indicator for Washington — it is a revenue variable.

A growing economy generates more taxable income and higher tax receipts from both corporate and individual taxpayers. Growing asset prices generate capital gains taxes. But those capital gains tax receipts can drop precipitously when asset prices drop.

The implication is that equity market prosperity is partially a fiscal policy outcome. A Fed posture that supports equity valuations — through lower rates and adequate liquidity — generates a fiscal dividend in the form of higher capital gains revenue, which in turn reduces the financing gap that requires additional Treasury issuance. The mechanism is indirect but real.

This creates a structural alignment between the Fed's interest in financial stability, the Treasury's interest in financing cost reduction, and the equity market's interest in accommodative monetary policy. All three benefit from lower rates and healthy asset prices. The risk, of course, is that this alignment creates its own distortion — that monetary policy becomes implicitly subordinate to equity market performance rather than independently focused on price stability and employment. That tension is real and unresolved, and it will define much of the debate about Warsh's tenure.

## Part VI: The Key Uncertainties

The Warsh framework is coherent on its own terms, but several variables could disrupt its execution.

**Inflation persistence.** Inflation jumped to a three-year high in April 2026, driven by energy price spikes from the Iran war. If that shock proves persistent rather than transitory, the case for rate cuts weakens substantially even under Warsh's trimmed-average inflation framework, which attempts to strip out extreme price moves for a cleaner read on underlying inflation. Warsh prefers this methodology precisely because it would allow rate cuts even while headline inflation is elevated by an energy shock — but markets and FOMC members may not accept that framing if the energy shock proves durable.

**FOMC coalition building.** The Fed chair controls the agenda but has only one vote. Warsh needs to build a majority for both balance sheet policy changes and rate cuts, in an FOMC that currently includes Biden-appointed governors, Trump-appointed governors, and regional bank presidents who are not presidential appointees. The most divided committee since 1992 will not move quickly on either front.

**Foreign demand fragility.** If geopolitical tensions — particularly around trade policy — cause foreign holders to reduce their Treasury purchases, the demand side of the Treasury market deteriorates precisely when supply is accelerating. This would push long yields up regardless of Fed balance sheet policy, complicating the yield curve dynamics that Warsh's framework depends upon.

**The Fed/Treasury accord question.** Warsh has referenced a potential new "Fed/Treasury accord" governing balance sheet management in ways he has not fully detailed. Former Fed officials have described his comments as "unclear or confusing at best" and "worrisome at worst." If interpreted aggressively, such an accord could blur the line between monetary and fiscal policy in ways that undermine the credibility that makes Warsh's framework viable in the first place.

---

## A Framework, Not a Forecast

The U.S. fiscal situation is not a problem that can be resolved through monetary policy alone. It is a structural mismatch between spending commitments and revenue capacity that has been building for decades and will require sustained political choices to address — choices that neither the Fed nor any single chair can compel.

What monetary policy can do is manage the transition: maintain Treasury market functioning, reduce where possible the cost of debt service, and preserve the equity market conditions that generate capital gains revenues. Warsh's rate-cut-plus-balance-sheet-reduction framework is the most coherent available approach for doing those things simultaneously.

Whether he can execute it depends on FOMC politics, inflation dynamics, and global capital flows that no single institution controls. The framework is sound. The execution will be the test.
