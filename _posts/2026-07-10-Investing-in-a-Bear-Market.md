Bear markets are not primarily a financial problem. They are a psychological one. The prices falling on your screen are real, but the decisions that destroy the most capital in a bear market are not caused by the decline itself — they are caused by the responses to it: the panic selling at the bottom, the premature re-entry chasing a dead-cat bounce, the capitulation into whatever narrative Wall Street is selling that week. The investors who come out of bear markets in the strongest position are almost never the ones who made the cleverest tactical moves. They are the ones who made the fewest mistakes.

What follows is the discipline I try to hold myself to when markets are falling and the pressure to act is highest.

---

## Principle 1: Patience Is the Position

The most underappreciated truth about bear markets: doing nothing is usually the highest-returning strategy available, and also the hardest to execute.

When prices fall day after day, inaction feels like paralysis. Every down day seems to demand a response — reduce exposure, hedge, rotate, do *something*. The impulse is understandable and almost always wrong. Investors who sell into drawdowns lock in losses and then face the even harder problem of deciding when to get back in, a decision they typically make too late, after much of the recovery has already happened.

The alternative is patience calibrated to evidence. Not indifference — active monitoring, analysis, and preparation — but a willingness to wait for the conditions that actually justify a position before deploying capital. In practice that means identifying the specific signals that would mark a durable low forming, and waiting for them instead of acting on the emotional urgency of the moment.

The single most important mindset shift in a bear market is time horizon extension. If you own businesses with durable competitive advantages — the quality you identified before the bear market began — a 30% price decline does not change the five-year outlook for those businesses. It changes the entry price available to new buyers. Patience, in that context, is not passivity. It is the active management of your own psychology in service of a longer-term thesis.

## Principle 2: Wait for RSI Oversold Before Adding — Not Before It

The Relative Strength Index is one of the most misused tools in retail investing. In bull markets, traders use RSI to time momentum entries. In bear markets, they use it to convince themselves the bottom is in because a stock they like has fallen far enough to *feel* cheap.

Neither is correct. RSI is most useful in bear markets as a patience discipline — a commitment to not adding to positions until selling pressure has become technically extended, no matter how attractive the fundamental case looks on its own.

A stock can be fundamentally undervalued and technically oversold at the same time. It can also be fundamentally undervalued with plenty of downside left before the selling exhausts itself. RSI below 30 — the conventional oversold threshold — signals that selling has been intense and sustained, that positioning is likely reduced, and that the risk/reward of a new entry has shifted meaningfully from what it was at higher prices.

What it doesn't tell you: whether the low is in, whether the thesis is intact, or whether the sector has found its floor. It tells you the *technical* setup is better than it was — that you are buying into exhausted selling rather than active distribution. Necessary, but not sufficient.

My practice: I use RSI below 30 on the weekly chart — not the daily, which is too noisy — as a filter for adding to existing positions or starting new ones in names where I have already done the fundamental work. The weekly RSI below 30 tells me the setup is technically favorable. The analysis I already completed tells me whether the business is worth buying. Both conditions have to hold.

The mistake: using RSI as a substitute for fundamental analysis rather than a complement to it. A terrible business with an RSI of 20 is still a terrible business. RSI oversold is a timing signal for quality names where the thesis is intact — not a rescue signal for broken businesses or positions you never understood.

## Principle 3: Maintain Critical Distance from Institutional Research

Dismissing Wall Street research entirely is as foolish as treating it as gospel. The reality is more specific: institutional research from the major banks serves purposes that are often misaligned with your interests as an individual investor, and you need to understand those purposes to read it correctly.

Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, and their peers produce research for several audiences at once: their trading desks, their prime brokerage clients, their investment banking relationships, and the general market. A published price target reflects a negotiation between what the analyst believes, what the banking relationship requires, what the trading desk is positioned for, and what compliance will approve. The output is not fabricated — but it is not purely analytical either.

Institutional price targets and earnings estimates are consensus products. They reflect what the aggregated research community thinks, calibrated to be defensible rather than bold. By definition, a consensus view is already in the price. If Goldman publishes a $200 target and the stock sits at $195, that target contains essentially no useful information. Returns come from holding a view that diverges from consensus in a direction that turns out to be right. Institutional research, by its nature, will never hand you that.

What it *is* good for: mapping the consensus range, seeing which risks the market is currently fixated on, tracking changes in analyst positioning over time — upgrades and downgrades measure shifts in institutional sentiment, not necessarily shifts in fundamental value — and cross-checking your own work.

The discipline: after reading any institutional note, ask two questions. First, what incentive does the author have to reach this conclusion? Second, what would this look like if I had never seen the price or the recent moves? If the first answer reveals a bias, and the second reveals a thesis built on extrapolating recent momentum, weight the note accordingly.

This is not cynicism. It is calibration. The most useful institutional research tends to be the most boring — long-form industry work, supply chain deep dives, channel checks — not the headline price targets and buy/sell calls that generate the commentary.

## Principle 4: Never Analyze a Single Stock in Isolation — Read the Entire Sector

One of the most reliable ways to lose money in a bear market is to fixate on a single name you like while ignoring the sector it sits in. A company can have exceptional fundamentals, a clean balance sheet, and flawless execution — and still fall 40% because its sector is undergoing institutional liquidation.

In a bear market, sector rotation and institutional positioning move prices more than individual fundamentals do. Sector ETF flows show what institutions are buying and selling at the portfolio level, and those flows push every stock in the sector regardless of company quality. Finding a high-quality name is the start of the analysis, not the end.

The framework I use:

First, place the sector in its cycle. Distribution — institutions reducing, typically falling prices on rising volume? Accumulation — quiet buying into weakness? Markup — broad retail and momentum participation in a rising trend? The sector ETF's chart structure, volume profile, and RSI say more about the risk/reward of a new position than any single-stock chart.

Second, check breadth. If the sector ETF is falling but a handful of quality names are holding up or falling less, that smells like selective institutional accumulation — constructive. If *everything* in the sector is falling by similar magnitudes, the problem is macro or institutional, not company-specific. Quality is temporarily irrelevant; patience is the correct response.

Third, find the relative strength leaders — the stocks in a beaten-down sector declining less than peers, or stabilizing first. These are usually the names institutional buyers prefer on the way down, and they tend to lead when the sector turns.

## Principle 5: Read the Macro Dashboard Before Reading Any Individual Chart

Stock and sector analysis lives inside a macro context that determines what it means. The same RSI reading means something different with the VIX at 15 than at 40. The same fundamental thesis plays out differently with a strengthening versus weakening dollar, wide versus narrow credit spreads, oil falling versus spiking.

The macro variables I check before any significant decision in a bear market:

**VIX — the volatility regime.** Above 30, institutional risk management is actively cutting equity exposure: forced selling, margin calls, risk-parity de-levering, all happening at once. Trying to catch a bottom there means fighting active institutional liquidation. Above 40 has historically marked peak fear and asymmetric opportunity — but peak fear is nearly impossible to act on without preparation done in advance. Below 20 in a recovering market means normalization. Between 20 and 30, the market is still deciding.

**SPX and QQQ — broad market structure.** No individual position deserves aggressive sizing with SPX below a declining 200-day moving average. The 200-day is not a magic line, but it works as an institutional positioning gauge: many systematic strategies cut equity exposure below it, which creates a self-reinforcing dynamic — being below the line invites more selling. The SPX–QQQ relationship matters too. Divergences, when tech leads or lags the broad market, show which sectors institutions are rotating into or out of.

**DXY and majors — the dollar flow gauge.** Dollar strength has historically meant tightening global liquidity: dollar-denominated debt gets more expensive to service, emerging-market capital flows reverse, commodities face headwinds. For a US-focused equity book, a strong dollar in a bear market usually lengthens the drawdown. For international exposure — Japanese equities, European names — currency moves can swamp business fundamentals over a 6–12 month horizon.

**Crude — the macro stress gauge.** Oil cuts both ways depending on direction and speed. Gradual increases in a growing economy signal demand strength. Sharp spikes from supply shocks — like the current Iran war energy shock — are stagflationary: higher input costs, compressed margins, weaker consumer purchasing power, and a harder inflation calculus for the Fed, all at once. Sharp *declines* in an already-stressed environment usually mean demand destruction — recession fears moving from narrative to data. Either extreme warns against adding equity risk.

I read all four together before interpreting any single position's price action. A stock technically oversold while the VIX sits above 35, SPX trades below its 200-day, the dollar strengthens, and oil spikes is a different animal from the same setup in a normalized macro environment. The first is a falling knife in a storm. The second is a genuine entry.

## Principle 6: The After-Hours and On-Chain Trap

Two behaviors destroy more retail capital in bear markets than almost anything else: trading off after-hours moves, and using real-time on-chain or crypto data as a leading indicator for broad risk positioning.

**The after-hours trap.** Overnight and pre-market moves happen in thin liquidity, where a few participants — often institutional algos reacting to headlines — can push prices hard on small volume. These moves frequently reverse or fade by the open, especially when the news is ambiguous or the move is an overreaction to a single data point. Entering or exiting on after-hours prints, before the regular session has processed the information with full liquidity, is systematically bad for retail.

The correct response to a big overnight move: note it, read the actual news, ask whether it changes the thesis, and wait for the regular session to show where real buyers and sellers meet.

**The on-chain trap.** Crypto market activity — exchange flows, on-chain transactions, perpetual futures open interest — tempts some investors as a real-time sentiment read on broader risk appetite. In bear markets this extrapolation is unreliable and dangerous.

Crypto trades 24/7 without the circuit breakers, settlement cycles, or regulatory framework of equities. A 3 AM Saturday selloff reflects a specific participant set — retail traders, levered perp holders, algo market makers — that does not represent the institutional investors who set equity direction. A sharp overnight crypto dump driven by a whale liquidation or a stablecoin redemption says essentially nothing about what SPX does at the NYSE open.

More to the point: using crypto price action as a leading indicator for equities inverts the correct hierarchy. Equities are the primary risk asset; their direction is set by macro, earnings, rates, and institutional positioning. Crypto is a high-volatility satellite that amplifies equity moves — larger in both directions — without leading them in any reliable way. Trading on crypto signals before equity sessions open is FOMO dressed up as analysis.

## The Integrated Decision Framework

My process, in practice:

1. **Check the macro dashboard first.** VIX, SPX vs. the 200-day, DXY direction, oil trend. Is the environment conducive to adding risk, or is institutional liquidation still running?

2. **Read the sector before the stock.** Where is the sector ETF structurally — distribution, accumulation, stabilization? What does breadth look like inside it?

3. **Confirm the thesis is intact.** Has the bear market changed the business's competitive position, balance sheet, or long-term earnings trajectory? Or has only the price changed?

4. **Check the weekly RSI.** Is the technical setup favorable — below 30 on the weekly — or is there room for further technical deterioration before selling exhausts?

5. **Size by conviction and macro uncertainty.** In a VIX-above-30 environment, size conservatively. In a normalizing environment with intact fundamentals and oversold technicals, size up toward the limits the portfolio framework sets.

6. **Do not act on after-hours moves or crypto signals.** Wait for the regular session. Wait for liquidity. Wait for the full participant set to process the information before reading the price signal.

7. **Ignore the headline research call.** Form your own view from the fundamental and technical picture. Read institutional research as context, not instruction.

The common thread: deliberately slowing down in an environment engineered to make you act fast. The bear market's main weapon against individual investors is urgency — the feeling that every move demands an immediate response. A pre-committed process beats that urgency by replacing it with a procedure that needs no real-time improvisation.

## A Final Note on Confidence Without Arrogance

Independent judgment runs through everything above. But independent judgment is not certainty. Bear markets are genuinely uncertain, and the most dangerous investor in one is the one most confident about where the bottom is.

The right epistemic stance: clear process, calibrated conviction, honest uncertainty about outcomes. I can believe a business is fundamentally attractive, the technical setup is improving, and the macro is turning from deterioration toward stabilization — and still size the position for the possibility that I am wrong on all three.

Position sizing is how a portfolio expresses uncertainty. Full conviction at full size assumes you are right. Scaled sizing assumes you might be wrong and keeps the ability to add as the thesis strengthens. In bear markets, where outcome distributions run wider than normal, scaled sizing is not timidity. It is the only intellectually honest way to hold a view on incomplete evidence — which is all evidence ever is.

The market will always know more than you do. The process is how you decide well anyway.

*The bear market rewards not the investor who acts most decisively, but the one who has prepared most thoroughly and acts most selectively.*
