There is a component at the heart of every major AI data center buildout that most people have never heard of. It is not the GPU. It is not the memory. It is the connection between them — the optical interconnect that carries data at the speed of light between chips, racks, and systems. And right now, the technology governing how those connections are made is undergoing a fundamental architectural shift.

That shift is called **Co-Packaged Optics**, or CPO. It is one of the most consequential enabling technologies in the current AI infrastructure cycle — and it is only beginning to move from engineering prototype to commercial deployment at scale.

---

## The Problem CPO Solves

To understand why CPO matters, you need to understand the problem it addresses.

Modern AI training clusters require massive, continuous data movement between GPUs, switches, and memory systems. As AI models have grown larger — from hundreds of billions to trillions of parameters — the volume of data that must flow between accelerators within a single training run has grown proportionally. The interconnect that handles this flow has become a critical bottleneck.

For decades, the dominant solution was the **pluggable optical transceiver** — a module that slots into a network switch or server, converts electrical signals to optical ones, and transmits them via fiber. These modules work well at moderate data rates. But as bandwidth requirements push through 800 gigabits per second toward 1.6 terabits and beyond, pluggable transceivers face two hard limits: **power consumption and signal integrity**.

Each pluggable transceiver consumes roughly 30 watts. A modern switch with hundreds of ports can require kilowatts just for the optical interface layer. And because the electrical signal must travel from the switch ASIC through a circuit board to reach the transceiver module, signal degradation at high data rates becomes increasingly difficult to manage.

CPO addresses bandwidth and latency bottlenecks by moving optical interfaces physically close to the electrical die, shortening high-speed electrical paths and enabling denser, lower-power optical fabrics. In a CPO design, the photonic components are integrated into the same package as the switching ASIC — eliminating the long electrical trace between chip and transceiver, reducing power consumption dramatically, and improving signal integrity at high data rates.

CPO significantly reduces power consumption from 15 to 5 picojoules per bit, with ambitions to go below 1. At the scale of a hyperscale AI data center, that efficiency difference is not marginal — it is the difference between a feasible power budget and one that exceeds what grid infrastructure can deliver.

## How CPO Works: The Technical Architecture

A conventional pluggable module is a self-contained unit: it houses lasers, modulators, photodetectors, and driver electronics in a removable form factor. The switch ASIC communicates with it over a high-speed electrical interface, and the module handles the conversion to optical.

In a CPO design, the optical components — typically built on a **silicon photonics** platform — are co-located with the switch ASIC inside the same package. The silicon photonics die contains waveguides, modulators, and photodetectors fabricated using standard semiconductor processes. An external laser source provides the light, which is coupled into the silicon photonics die and modulated to carry data.

NVIDIA's Quantum-X photonic switch delivers 115.2 terabits per second total throughput using two CPO modules. Each module houses a Quantum-X800 ASIC built on TSMC's 4N process with 107 billion transistors and six optical components including 18 silicon photonic engines. The 200 gigabits per second micro-ring modulators achieve a 3.5x power reduction.

NVIDIA introduced two new networking switch platforms at GTC 2025 — Spectrum X Photonics and Quantum X Photonics — both built on co-packaged optics. Central to these platforms is TSMC's System on Integrated Chips technology, which provides the 3D integration infrastructure. The SoIC X variant, TSMC's advanced bumpless hybrid bonding process, enables vertical stacking of logic dies and other heterogeneous components at sub-ten micrometer pitch — dramatically shortening interconnect length and reducing resistance and latency.

The physics here are straightforward: shorter electrical paths mean less resistance, less capacitance, less signal degradation, and lower power. CPO is not a speculative technology — it is applied physics solving a real engineering constraint.

## The Three Demand Vectors

CPO's growth story is not driven by a single application. Three distinct and reinforcing demand sources are pulling the technology into production simultaneously.

### AI Data Centers

This is the primary driver today. The United States hosts the majority of the world's frontier AI training clusters, many of which are equipped with NVIDIA H100 and upcoming B200/GB200 GPU systems that demand 800G and 1.6T CPO interconnects.

The scale of hyperscaler capital commitments makes the demand structural rather than cyclical. Microsoft announced an $80 billion AI infrastructure investment plan for fiscal year 2026, Google committed over $75 billion in 2025, and Meta projected $60–65 billion in capital expenditure for 2025, with meaningful portions allocated to next-generation optical networking.

TrendForce estimates that CPO shipments will exceed 50 million by 2030, increasing CPO's penetration rate within optical networking from less than 1% to more than 35%. This is not incremental growth — it is a technology transition at the architectural level.

### Advanced Chips and HPC

High-performance computing and next-generation chip architectures require the same high-bandwidth, low-latency interconnects as AI training clusters. As chips continue to scale — both in die count per package (chiplets) and in cluster size (multi-rack systems) — the electrical interconnect bottleneck becomes universal across computing workloads, not just AI.

HPC and AI/ML clusters are expected to grow at a 36.96% CAGR over the forecast period — outpacing even the already-high overall CPO market growth rate, reflecting the particularly intense interconnect demands of large-scale compute.

### Robotics and Autonomy

This is the least appreciated demand vector for CPO, but potentially the most significant over a longer horizon.

Physical AI — robots, autonomous vehicles, drone swarms — requires real-time data processing with constraints that are fundamentally different from data center AI. Latency is not a performance metric; it is a safety constraint. A humanoid robot or autonomous vehicle making physical decisions in a dynamic environment cannot tolerate millisecond delays introduced by inefficient optical interconnects at the sensor-to-compute interface.

As robotics scales from laboratory prototypes to factory floors and logistics networks, the data throughput and latency requirements of on-board compute will push against the same electrical interconnect limits that drove CPO adoption in data centers. The form factors will differ — CPO for robotics requires miniaturization and ruggedization that data center CPO does not — but the underlying physics problem is identical.

IDTechEx projects the CPO market to exceed $20 billion by 2036, growing at a CAGR of 37% from 2026 to 2036. Robotics and autonomy applications are a meaningful component of that long-term projection.

## The Market Transition: From Prototype to Production

CPO has been a topic of industry discussion for nearly a decade. What has changed in 2025–2026 is that it has transitioned from engineering prototype to commercial production — a distinction with significant implications for investors.

In January 2026, Broadcom confirmed shipments of more than 50,000 Tomahawk 5-Bailly CPO switches during 2025 and previewed its 200G-per-lane third-generation platform.

In November 2025, NVIDIA unveiled Quantum-X800 InfiniBand, integrating 144 ports of 800G CPO and claiming a 63-fold signal-integrity gain versus OSFP modules.

2026 is expected to see the initial volume ramp of CPO on both InfiniBand and Ethernet switches, with major hyperscalers actively trialing the technology.

The transition from trial to volume ramp is the inflection point that transforms a promising technology into a durable revenue stream. The industry is at that inflection now.

## Investment Landscape: Where Value Is Accumulating

The CPO supply chain has several distinct layers, each with different risk and return profiles.

### Vertically Integrated Platform Players

**NVIDIA (NVDA)** and **Broadcom (AVGO)** are the two companies with the most direct CPO exposure at the platform level. Both are integrating CPO into their switch and networking products — NVIDIA through Quantum-X and Spectrum-X, Broadcom through its Tomahawk/Bailly platform.

Broadcom, NVIDIA, and Marvell secure most hyperscale sockets by vertically integrating switch silicon and photonics. This vertical integration is a structural advantage: it allows platform players to optimize the entire signal chain from ASIC to fiber, rather than relying on the performance of standalone third-party modules.

For NVIDIA specifically, CPO is not just a product feature — it is a supply chain strategy. On March 2, 2026, NVIDIA announced a $4 billion investment split between Coherent and Lumentum — $2 billion into each — tied to multi-year purchase commitments and capacity access aimed at scaling co-packaged optics. Writing $4 billion in optical supply chain commitments is not a hedge; it is a declaration that CPO is central to NVIDIA's next-generation AI infrastructure roadmap.

### Optical Component Pure Plays

**Coherent (COHR)** and **Lumentum (LITE)** are the two largest pure-play optical component companies with direct CPO exposure. Both are suppliers to NVIDIA and are now backed by NVIDIA's multi-year purchase commitments.

Lumentum holds what may be the strongest hand in the laser component market. It is currently the only supplier shipping 200G-per-lane EMLs at volume — the critical component for next-generation 1.6T pluggable transceivers. Its CEO has called 2026 "a breakout year for laser chip sales."

Lumentum's revenue rose by over 65% year-over-year in its latest quarter, with adjusted operating margin expanding by 1,730 basis points.

The investment case for optical pure plays is compelling but concentrated: much of their near-term revenue is tied to NVIDIA's deployment pace, which means upside is high when NVIDIA executes and risk is asymmetric when it does not.

### Semiconductor and Photonics Infrastructure

**Marvell (MRVL)** occupies an interesting position in the CPO ecosystem. In December 2025, Marvell signed a deal to acquire Celestial AI for up to $5.5 billion — a bet that optical interconnects will become standard plumbing for every AI data center. Marvell's Teralynx CPO platform provides an alternative to NVIDIA's and Broadcom's vertically integrated offerings, serving hyperscalers that prefer multi-vendor supply chains.

**TSMC** is the foundational enabler that most CPO analyses underweight. Other major players, including Broadcom, have adopted TSMC's COUPE platform, underscoring the growing importance of 3D integration and hybrid bonding in CPO. TSMC's advanced packaging capabilities — SoIC, CoWoS, and COUPE — are prerequisites for the heterogeneous integration that CPO requires. Without TSMC's packaging roadmap, the CPO roadmaps of NVIDIA, Broadcom, and Marvell do not execute.

**Fabrinet (FN)** serves as the contract manufacturer to the laser and transceiver ecosystem — a lower-profile but operationally critical position in the supply chain.

## Risks Worth Understanding

The CPO thesis is strong, but three risks deserve honest assessment.

**Technology transition risk.** CPO requires new switch architectures, different cabling infrastructure, updated standards, and changes to data center design. Adoption requires new switch architectures, cabling, and standards. Large-scale CPO deployments are projected between 2028 and 2030. The transition will take time, and near-term revenue forecasts across multiple research firms vary significantly — reflecting genuine uncertainty about the adoption pace.

**Supplier concentration.** Much of the optical market is tied to a single customer, NVIDIA, which is rolling out new architectural iterations at an unusually fast pace. Companies deeply integrated into NVIDIA's CPO supply chain benefit when NVIDIA executes on schedule and face disproportionate risk when product cycles slip or architectural choices change.

**Standards fragmentation.** CPO is not yet a fully standardized technology. Multiple integration approaches — on-board, socketed, fully integrated — are competing, and hyperscalers have different preferences. The eventual convergence on dominant standards will create winners and losers among current suppliers.

## The Structural View

Market size projections for CPO vary across research firms, reflecting the genuine uncertainty of a market in early commercial deployment. The global CPO market is expected to grow from approximately $95–120 million in 2025 to over $1 billion by 2034, at a CAGR of approximately 30–36%. IDTechEx projects the market to exceed $20 billion by 2036 at a 37% CAGR. The range in projections is wide — but the direction is not in dispute.

What is consistent across every credible forecast: CPO penetration in AI data center optical networking will go from negligible today to dominant within a decade. The physics argument is settled. The technology is in production. The capital commitments are signed.

The remaining question — always the harder one — is timing and positioning. Which companies capture the value as the transition accelerates? Which are squeezed by the shift from pluggable to co-packaged? Which platform architectures win the hyperscaler qualification races that determine multi-year revenue streams?

Those questions do not have clean answers today. But the investment case for CPO as a structural theme — not a trade, but a multi-year infrastructure transition with compounding demand from AI, chips, and robotics — is among the more clearly articulated opportunities in the current technology landscape.
