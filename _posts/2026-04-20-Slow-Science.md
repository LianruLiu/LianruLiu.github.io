“90% of scientific research is crap.”  
This sounds like an attack from outside academia. It is not.  
It comes from Adrian Barnett, a professor of statistics and meta-researcher at Queensland University of Technology, who published a blog post titled *“90% of scientific research is crap”* in January 2025. One year later, in January 2026, he wrote in *Nature* that he would halve his own publication output and encouraged others to consider “slow science” as well.   
Barnett’s argument is not anti-science. It is the opposite. It is a defense of science against the industrialization of academic production.

## 1. The Problem Is Not That Science Fails  
Science must fail. Failed experiments, negative results, weak hypotheses, and dead ends are part of the process.  
The real problem is different:  
> The academic system increasingly rewards the appearance of productivity rather than the production of knowledge.  
Barnett invokes Sturgeon’s Law: “90% of everything is crap.” He applies it to scientific research not as a joke, but as a diagnostic tool.  
A similar concern appeared in a 2009 *Lancet* paper by Iain Chalmers and Paul Glasziou, who estimated that around 85% of health and medical research investment was avoidably wasted due to poor research questions, weak design, non-publication, and incomplete reporting. 

## 2. Research Waste Is Not Just Financial Waste  
If global biomedical research spending reaches hundreds of billions of dollars, an 85% waste rate implies an enormous misallocation of resources.  
But money is only the visible part.  
Research waste also means:  
- Researchers spend years on low-value work.
- Patients wait for evidence that never becomes reliable.
- Policymakers make decisions on weak foundations.
- Public trust in science becomes more fragile.

The deeper cost is epistemic: society produces more papers, but not necessarily more understanding.

## 3. The Publication System Is Expanding Faster Than It Can Think  
Barnett’s 2026 *Nature* article points to a system under severe pressure. Publication volume has grown rapidly, and peer review has not scaled at the same speed.  
This is not merely a problem of “too many papers.” A larger scientific community naturally produces more research.  
The problem is that the incentive structure encourages researchers to publish constantly, even when the marginal contribution is small.  
This produces a dangerous equilibrium:  
> More papers create more review burden.  
> More review burden lowers review quality.  
> Lower review quality allows more weak papers through.  
> More weak papers further increase the burden.

This is not a temporary inefficiency. It is a structural feedback loop.

## 4. Hyper-Prolific Publishing Reveals the Absurdity  
Some researchers now publish dozens of papers per year. Studies of hyper-prolific authors commonly use thresholds such as more than 60 papers annually — roughly one paper every few days.  
Of course, collaboration can explain part of this. Large teams, data consortia, and multi-author projects can produce legitimate high output.  
But the question remains:  
> At what point does authorship stop meaning intellectual responsibility?  
A high-quality paper requires reading, designing, collecting data, analyzing, writing, revising, and responding to critique.  
If someone publishes at industrial speed, the system must ask whether authorship has become a credentialing device rather than a record of intellectual contribution.  

## 5. Peer Review Is Becoming the Bottleneck  
Mark Hanson and co-authors describe the “strain on scientific publishing”: scientists are increasingly overwhelmed by the volume of papers being produced, while reviewing and editorial capacity remain limited.  
This matters because peer review is the quality-control layer of modern science.  
If that layer becomes overloaded, the system does not simply slow down. It starts accepting more noise.  
The result is not only fraud. Fraud is serious, but it is not the whole problem.  
The larger problem may be the mass production of papers that are technically real but intellectually thin.  

## 6. The Crisis Is an Incentive Problem  
It is tempting to blame individual researchers.  
But Barnett’s deeper point is that the system creates the behavior it later condemns.  
Researchers are told:  
- Publish more.
- Get cited more.
- Aim for high-impact journals.
- Build a longer CV.
- Compete for grants.
- Survive promotion committees.  
Then the same system complains that researchers optimize for quantity.  
This is not hypocrisy at the individual level. It is institutional design failure.  
> When careers are measured by output volume, output volume becomes the product.  

## 7. Metrics Have Replaced Judgment  
The obsession with journal impact factors, h-indexes, citation counts, and university rankings creates a false sense of objectivity.  
These metrics are convenient because they are easy to count.  
But easy-to-count is not the same as meaningful.  
A paper’s value should depend on what it discovers, clarifies, corrects, or enables — not merely where it appears.  
Barnett’s response is symbolic but powerful: he removed journal names from his CV, leaving only titles, authors, and years. The message is clear:  
> Read the work. Do not outsource judgment to the journal brand.

## 8. Slow Science Is Not Lazy Science  
Barnett’s decision to halve his publication output is not a retreat from research.  
It is a decision to spend more time on each paper:  
- reading the background literature more deeply;
- consulting stakeholders;
- testing assumptions;
- validating models;
- improving clarity;
- asking whether the work actually matters.

This is the core of slow science.  
Slow science does not mean doing less work.  
It means doing less performative work.  
It means replacing speed with depth.

## 9. The Best Career May Contain Only a Few Truly Important Papers  
Barnett offers young researchers a surprisingly liberating expectation: if they finish their career with two or three genuine breakthroughs, that is already extraordinary.  
This is a healthier view of academic life.  
A career should not be judged by the total number of papers produced, but by the number of ideas that remain useful after the noise has disappeared.  
Most papers will be forgotten.  
A few will change how people think.  
That is enough.

## 10. My View: Science Needs De-Leveraging  
The academic system has become over-leveraged.  
Not financially, but intellectually.  
Too many careers are built on thin increments.  
Too many institutions reward volume.  
Too many journals monetize expansion.  
Too many researchers are forced to optimize for survival rather than truth.  
Slow science is a form of intellectual deleveraging.  
It asks academia to reduce output pressure, restore judgment, and rebuild quality control before the publication system collapses under its own weight.  
The goal is not fewer ideas.  
The goal is fewer meaningless papers, so that meaningful ideas can breathe.

## Conclusion: Less Output, More Knowledge  
Barnett’s argument is uncomfortable because it attacks the academic system from within.  
He is not saying science is worthless.  
He is saying science is too important to be buried under its own production machine.  
The future of research may not depend on publishing faster.  
It may depend on having the courage to publish less, think longer, and make each contribution matter.  
> Science does not need more noise.  
> It needs stronger signals.
