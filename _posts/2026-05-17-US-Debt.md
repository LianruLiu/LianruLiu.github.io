Kevin Warsh was confirmed as Fed Chair on May 13, 2026, in a 54–45 vote — the most partisan confirmation in Fed history. He inherits an institution under extraordinary pressure: structural fiscal dysfunction, inflation running hot on a Middle East energy shock, and a Treasury market whose plumbing is quietly getting more fragile. What Warsh is likely to do — and why — starts with the fiscal trap that constrains every option he has.

---

## Part I: The Fiscal Problem — Debt Is Now the Dominant Budget Item

The numbers aren't abstract anymore. As of early 2026, total U.S. public debt is roughly $38.6 trillion. Interest payments hit a new record in 2025, well above $1 trillion a year. In Q4 2025 alone: $307 billion — 31.6% of quarterly tax receipts. Debt service is now the largest and fastest-growing line in the federal budget, crowding out everything else.

The CBO projects debt held by the public rising from 101% of GDP in 2026 to 120% by 2036 — and 175% by 2056 on current trajectories, even counting revenue from new tariff regimes. These aren't tail-risk scenarios. They're the baseline.

The arithmetic is straightforward: the federal government runs a deficit of about 6% of GDP a year — roughly double the historical norm. That deficit needs continuous new Treasury issuance, which feeds a debt stock that compounds interest payments, which widens the deficit further. The feedback loop doesn't self-correct under current policy.

That's the fiscal backdrop for every monetary policy decision. It constrains the Fed's room to maneuver — and it defines what "independent" monetary policy can actually mean in practice.

---

## Part II: Who Holds the Debt — and Why It Matters

The Treasury market's stability rests entirely on a diverse set of holders willing to absorb continuous issuance. Who holds what — and when their behavior might change — is where the fragility lives.

### Short-Term Treasury Bills: Corporations, Banks, and Money Market Funds

T-bills (up to one year) sit mostly with domestic commercial banks, corporations managing cash, and money market funds. Their demand is yield-driven — especially relative to the Fed's policy rate. When the Fed cuts short rates, T-bill yields fall, and T-bills get less attractive against other short-duration paper.

The linkage runs both ways: Fed rate cuts reduce the government's interest bill on $6 trillion of outstanding T-bills — near-term fiscal relief. But they can also push yield-sensitive holders toward alternatives. A delicate balance.

### Foreign Holders: The Currency Swap Dimension

As of December 2025, foreign entities hold about $9.2 trillion in Treasuries — 31% of publicly held debt. Japan is the largest single foreign holder at $1.24 trillion, then the UK at $897 billion, then China at $693 billion.

Japan deserves the attention. Japanese banks, insurers, and pension funds are among the most important marginal buyers of Treasuries, and their behavior tracks the USD/JPY exchange rate and the Bank of Japan's policy path. A sharply weaker yen means currency losses on dollar assets; a stronger yen, the reverse. Fed–BOJ currency swap lines exist precisely to manage the dollar-liquidity side of this interconnection.

One more thing: foreign holders' Treasury positions and their equity exposures aren't independent. Japanese and European institutions typically hold U.S. Treasuries and U.S. equities as one integrated portfolio. A Treasury dislocation that forces foreign holders to sell creates correlated pressure across both asset classes. Bonds and equities aren't as separate as they look in analysis. The wiring runs both directions.

### Medium- and Long-Term Bonds: Insurance Companies, Pension Funds, and Structural Erosion

The 10-year and 30-year have always belonged to insurers and pension funds — institutions with long-dated liabilities that want matching long-duration assets. Their demand is structural, driven by asset-liability management, not return-chasing.

But that buyer base has a demographic problem. As the U.S. ages, pension obligations come due and insurance claims rise. The aggregate asset pool grows more slowly — in some cases shrinks — as payouts outrun contributions. The structural bid for long-duration Treasuries is contracting relative to new supply. Slow-moving, but real.

### Hedge Funds and the Liquidity Backstop

With the traditional long-bond buyer base under demographic pressure, hedge funds have become a bigger liquidity source in intermediate and long Treasuries — basis trades, relative value, outright directional bets. Leveraged money now absorbs more supply and provides more market-making capacity.

That matters because hedge fund participation is conditional in a way insurer demand isn't. A hedge fund absorbs supply when the risk/return works; it withdraws — fast — when it doesn't. In stress, when real-money demand is most needed, leveraged buyers are usually de-risking at the same time. Liquidity provision in normal markets becomes liquidity withdrawal in stressed ones. March 2020 showed it vividly: even benchmark Treasuries got hard to trade at sane prices.

## Part III: Why Rate Hikes Are Off the Table

With inflation above target and an energy shock from the Iran war, the textbook case says hike. Inflation is over 2%; tightening would cool demand.

The case collapses once fiscal arithmetic walks in.

The government refinances enormous volumes of debt continuously. As Treasuries mature and roll at market rates, the average rate on the debt ratchets up. Every 100 basis points on $38 trillion adds roughly $380 billion a year in interest expense — an entire discretionary spending category. Debt service already eats nearly a third of tax receipts. A serious hiking cycle would compound that burden in a way that demands either dramatic spending cuts (politically implausible), big tax increases (similarly constrained), or monetization (inflationary — the opposite of the goal).

Then there's the equity channel, which runs straight into fiscal revenues. Capital gains taxes are among the most volatile and economically significant pieces of federal receipts. Rising markets generate taxable realizations; a hiking-induced selloff does the reverse. Washington isn't a passive observer of equity performance — it's a direct fiscal beneficiary. An aggressive hiking cycle would cut capital gains revenue exactly when deficit pressure is worst.

Hikes aren't literally impossible — if inflation gets deeply entrenched and the Middle East deteriorates further, the FOMC could be forced. But in anything resembling the current picture, rate hikes are a lever the fiscal position can't afford to pull at scale. CME FedWatch prices a 97% chance of no change at Warsh's first meeting in June, with steady rates the base case for the rest of 2026.

## Part IV: The Warsh Framework — Rate Cuts Plus Balance Sheet Reduction

Warsh has laid out a distinctive framework, and it's internally coherent — though it breaks with the conventional pairing of rate cuts and balance sheet expansion.

The conventional view treats cuts and QE as complements: to ease, do both. Warsh inverts it: a smaller balance sheet and a lower policy rate can coexist — and the former enables the latter.

His logic: the Fed's large balance sheet has distorted the long end, suppressed term premium, and kept financial conditions artificially loose at the long end even with a restrictive policy rate at the short end. Shrinking the balance sheet — letting long-duration assets roll off — normalizes the long end, restores term premium, and re-anchors the curve to actual risk. That creates room for a lower policy rate without loose financial conditions.

He's said it directly: "In a way, one can afford a lower interest rate with a smaller balance sheet." QT and rate cuts aren't contradictory in this framing — they're complementary tools aimed at different parts of the curve.

Operationally, the playbook likely runs:

**More short-term bills on the Fed's balance sheet.** The Fed has already started buying T-bills to keep bank reserves adequate. Expanding that — more short-duration holdings, fewer long-duration ones — shrinks the Fed's long-bond footprint without a reserve shortage that would destabilize short-term funding. Lower systemic liquidity risk from balance sheet reduction.

**Commercial banks absorbing more medium-duration Treasuries.** As the Fed's long-bond holdings shrink, banks are the natural replacement bid. Banks hold large reserves earning interest-on-reserve; when the spread is attractive, redeploying into Treasuries pays. Regulatory tweaks — especially to the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) — could expand banks' capacity to hold Treasuries without hitting capital constraints.

**Cutting the short-term policy rate.** With the balance sheet normalizing and long rates re-anchored to genuine risk, there's room to cut the policy rate without re-igniting inflation. Lower short rates cut the government's T-bill borrowing costs, relieve rate-sensitive sectors, and — critically — support equity valuations through the discounting mechanism.

Warsh has been clear about the constraints: balance sheet reduction "isn't something that can or should happen quickly," needs FOMC consensus rather than chair fiat, and will "take time, patience, and care." Every actual move needs a committee vote — and this is the most divided FOMC since 1992, with four dissenters at the April meeting.

## Part V: The Equity Market as Fiscal Infrastructure

The equity market's role in federal fiscal health gets understated in most debt-sustainability discussion.

Capital gains taxes are among the most market-sensitive components of federal revenue. Strong returns and realized gains mean surging receipts; market declines mean they collapse. Equity performance isn't just an economic indicator for Washington — it's a revenue variable.

A growing economy generates more taxable income from corporations and individuals alike. Rising asset prices generate capital gains taxes. But those receipts can fall off a cliff when asset prices drop.

So equity market prosperity is partly a fiscal policy outcome. A Fed posture that supports valuations — lower rates, adequate liquidity — pays a fiscal dividend in capital gains revenue, which narrows the financing gap that requires more Treasury issuance. Indirect, but real.

That creates a structural alignment: the Fed wants financial stability, the Treasury wants lower financing costs, the equity market wants accommodative policy. All three benefit from lower rates and healthy asset prices. The risk is that the alignment distorts — that monetary policy becomes implicitly subordinate to equity performance instead of independently targeting price stability and employment. That tension is real, unresolved, and will define much of the debate over Warsh's tenure.

## Part VI: The Key Uncertainties

The framework is coherent on its own terms. Several variables could still break its execution.

**Inflation persistence.** Inflation hit a three-year high in April 2026 on energy spikes from the Iran war. If that shock persists rather than fades, the case for cuts weakens — even under Warsh's trimmed-average inflation framework, which strips extreme price moves for a cleaner read on underlying inflation. He prefers that methodology precisely because it permits cuts while headline inflation runs hot on energy. Markets and FOMC members may not buy the framing if the shock proves durable.

**FOMC coalition building.** The chair sets the agenda but gets one vote. Warsh needs a majority for both balance sheet moves and rate cuts — from a committee mixing Biden appointees, Trump appointees, and regional bank presidents who answer to no president. The most divided committee since 1992 won't move fast on either front.

**Foreign demand fragility.** If geopolitics — trade policy especially — pushes foreign holders to slow Treasury purchases, demand deteriorates just as supply accelerates. Long yields rise regardless of Fed balance sheet policy, and the yield-curve dynamics Warsh's framework needs get a lot harder.

**The Fed/Treasury accord question.** Warsh has floated a potential new "Fed/Treasury accord" on balance sheet management without fully detailing it. Former Fed officials have called his comments "unclear or confusing at best" and "worrisome at worst." Read aggressively, such an accord could blur monetary and fiscal policy in ways that destroy the credibility the framework depends on.

---

## A Framework, Not a Forecast

Monetary policy alone can't fix the U.S. fiscal position. It's a structural gap between spending commitments and revenue capacity, decades in the making, and closing it takes political choices no Fed chair can compel.

What monetary policy can do is manage the transition: keep the Treasury market functioning, trim debt-service costs where possible, and preserve the equity conditions that generate capital gains revenue. Warsh's rate-cut-plus-balance-sheet-reduction framework is the most coherent available way to do all three at once.

Whether he can execute it comes down to FOMC politics, inflation dynamics, and global capital flows — none of which any single institution controls. The framework is sound. The execution is the test.
