I didn't build my investment framework from theory. I built it from mistakes — expensive enough to teach, early enough to fix. The pattern, once I traced it: I lost money whenever I moved before I understood, and made money whenever I waited until I did. The framework below is that observation, extended and stress-tested into rules I try not to break.

This isn't a system for generating alpha through information asymmetry or quant models. It's a system for avoiding the errors that destroy most individual investors' long-term returns: acting on incomplete understanding, chasing momentum, and letting the fear of missing out substitute for analysis.

Here's the framework, as I actually use it.

---

## Principle 1: The Supply Chain Is the Unit of Analysis — Not the Stock

The most consistent mistake I made early was treating the company as the unit of analysis. I'd research a business — revenue, margins, management — and build a thesis on whether it would grow. Necessary, but not enough. What I was missing was the *system* around it: who supplied it, who it sold to, what regulated it, what could replace it, and where value actually accumulated in its industry.

The supply chain is the real unit of analysis. Every industry is a structure — raw material suppliers, component makers, system integrators, distribution, end customers, regulators — and value doesn't spread evenly across it. It concentrates at the points of scarcity, irreplaceability, or structural leverage. Finding those points comes before asking whether any specific company is cheap or expensive.

**The practical method:** Before investing in any company, I map its industry vertically. Who are the tier-1 and tier-2 suppliers? Where are gross margins highest in the chain, and why? Who has pricing power — the ability to raise prices without losing customers — and where does it come from? Proprietary technology, regulatory licensing, network effects, switching costs, or just capital intensity that keeps competitors out?

This exercise often gives a counterintuitive answer: the best investment in an industry isn't the company you started with. It's a supplier two levels upstream, or an infrastructure provider whose customer base is the entire industry. When I first formed views on AI infrastructure, my instinct was to focus on the model companies. The supply chain pointed to semiconductors, advanced packaging, and power infrastructure instead. Less glamorous, more durable.

The supply chain lens also defends against growth narratives. A company can post extraordinary revenue growth while being structurally disadvantaged in its chain — creating value at the top of the income statement while suppliers extract it or competitors erode it at the margin level. Revenue growth is not investable value. The supply chain tells you which is which.

## Principle 2: Structure the Portfolio Before Entering Any Position

Most individual investors build portfolios the way they furnish apartments: one piece at a time, whatever catches their eye, no plan for how the pieces fit together. The result is unintentional concentration, unexamined correlations, and no logic for how the whole thing behaves in different environments.

I did this for longer than I should have. I had conviction on individual names but had never asked the prior question: what kind of portfolio am I building, and how does each new position fit?

**The framework I use before adding anything:**

*What is the thesis category?* I run three buckets. First, infrastructure bets — long-duration, early-stage positions in structural transitions that take five to ten years to play out (quantum computing, satellite internet, new energy architecture). Sized to survive being early and wrong for years. Second, quality compounders — durable competitive advantages, compounding through earnings growth over a full cycle. Third, cyclical opportunities — shorter-duration positions in quality businesses temporarily mispriced by rotation, earnings misses, or macro headwinds. Each bucket has its own holding period, sizing logic, and definition of "working."

*How does it interact with what I already own?* I check explicitly for sector concentration, factor concentration (another high-multiple growth stock when the portfolio already carries duration risk), and thematic concentration (a second semiconductor name on the same thesis). Correlation hides behind sector labels — two companies in different industries can move together if they share the same economic driver.

*What is the sizing logic?* Size should reflect conviction *and* the cost of being wrong. A binary outcome — spectacular win or near-total loss — gets sized differently from a stable business where the downside is a few years of underperformance. My rule is simple: no single position going to zero should change my financial life in a material way. Above that floor, sizing scales with conviction and the quality of my informational edge.

*What is the exit framework?* I decide before entering when I would sell. This stops the slow drift into being "married" to a position and rationalizing the hold as the thesis decays. Three legitimate exits: the thesis is wrong (something I believed turns out false), the price has reached fair value or beyond (the opportunity is realized), or a better opportunity appeared and capital should move. Price decline alone is not a reason to sell when the thesis is intact — and that distinction is one of the most practically important in portfolio management.

## Principle 3: Do Not Confuse FOMO with a Thesis

Fear of missing out is the most expensive emotional state in investing. It's also the most disguised: FOMO almost always arrives dressed as a thesis. You catch yourself articulating reasons to buy — large market, exceptional management, transformative technology — while the real driver is that the stock already went up and you don't want to be the person who missed it.

I've made this mistake enough to know its signature: the reasoning comes *after* the emotional pull, not before. When I notice I'm building a case for something I first encountered because it was rising, that's a warning signal.

**The diagnostic questions:**

*Would I want this company if the stock had been flat for three years?* Plenty of great businesses are genuinely attractive after years of flat prices and investor disinterest. FOMO purchases usually involve businesses that got attention because their price went up. If my interest depends on recent momentum, the case is weaker than it looks.

*What changed in the business that wasn't known or valued twelve months ago?* If a stock is up 80% in a year, I want to know what specifically changed in the underlying economics — not the narrative, the reality — to justify the rerating. If the honest answer is "not much," the move is sentiment, and the risk/reward for a new entry is worse than it appears.

*Am I comfortable owning it if the price goes back to where it was six months ago?* If a 30–40% pullback would break my thesis or my stomach, I'm not buying value — I'm buying momentum. Momentum can work, but it needs different discipline and different exits than value investing. Mixing the two unconsciously is a reliable way to collect the downside of both and the upside of neither.

The practical result: I have a standing bias *against* initiating positions after strong recent performance, and *toward* sectors that are out of favor. That doesn't mean buying weakness reflexively — declining stocks are often declining for good reasons — but my default entry favors patience over urgency.

## Principle 4: Growth Phases Demand Caution; Drawdowns Demand Preparation

This principle captures my temperament most directly, and it runs against conventional investment culture, which celebrates participation in strong markets and treats caution as timidity.

My experience is the reverse. My most durable returns came from entering during price weakness, investor disinterest, or sector pessimism — in businesses with intact or improving fundamentals. My worst outcomes came from entering what was already well-known, widely owned, and priced for continued strength.

**During growth phases and strong markets:**

I don't stop investing in bull markets, but I raise my standards. The question isn't "will this business keep growing" — many will — but "how much of that growth is already in the price, and what surprise is needed to earn a decent return from here?"

Late in a cycle, valuations expand with confidence, and it gets harder to find quality the market has underestimated. My response: concentrate existing positions, raise cash modestly, and avoid new entries that need multiple expansion to work. I'll underperform the final innings of a bull market in exchange for smaller drawdowns and better entries in the next cycle.

That's psychologically difficult. Lagging a rising market feels like being wrong even when the logic is sound. The urge to add the position "just for now" never fully goes away. I've learned to treat that urge as a signal, not as guidance.

**During drawdowns and sector-specific stress:**

Drawdowns are when I do my most serious fundamental work. What interests me most is the high-quality business in a temporarily impaired sector — durable advantages, strong balance sheet, intact long-term thesis, trading at a discount because the sector fell out of favor, a macro headwind hit near-term earnings, or sentiment smeared legitimate worries about weak competitors across the whole group.

My entry checklist: the business proved its competitive position through a previous cycle; the balance sheet survives a long downturn without dilutive raises; management communicates clearly and consistently (evasive communication under stress is a tell); and the valuation reflects pessimism, not realistic earnings power.

I'll buy something that keeps falling after I buy it, as long as the thesis holds. "The price went down" and "I was wrong" are different statements, and confusing them produces two equal and opposite errors: selling good businesses in temporary drawdowns, and holding bad ones as fundamentals rot.

**The asymmetry I'm trying to exploit:**

Most investors are wired to do the opposite — more comfortable buying strength than weakness, more comfortable selling in drawdowns than in rallies. That behavioral asymmetry is well documented, and it creates persistent mispricing that patient investors can exploit with no informational edge at all. The edge isn't analytical. It's temperamental.

## Principle 5: The Cost of Premature Entry Is Underappreciated

There's a specific impatience that's expensive and common among investors who've done real research: entering before the business reaches the stage where the market can validate the thesis.

I've done this repeatedly with early-stage tech infrastructure — right about the structural transition, too early on the maturation curve for it to show up in earnings, then watching the position bleed for two or three years before anything happened. Right thesis, wrong timing. And holding a loser while the market rises carries a psychological cost: it pressures you to exit exactly when the thesis is nearest its inflection.

**Questions I ask before entering early-stage positions:**

*What moves this from "thesis" to "evidence"?* A thesis is a belief about the future; evidence is what the market reprices on. Naming the specific milestones — product launches, customer wins, regulatory approvals, earnings inflections — that will start the repricing gives a more disciplined entry than "the long-term story is compelling."

*How long can I hold without validation, and am I sized for it?* Early-stage infrastructure positions should be sized to survive a three-to-five-year wait without warping the rest of the portfolio. If a position is too big to hold comfortably through years of no feedback, it will get sold at the worst possible moment.

*Is there an existing cash-flow business I can own while I wait?* The same structural transition that creates early-stage opportunity usually creates nearer-term opportunity in established beneficiaries. Owning the established name provides stability and return while the early-stage position develops — and it funds the patience that early-stage investing requires.

## Principle 6: Know What You Own Well Enough to Defend It Under Pressure

This is the principle that holds the others together. Every framework gets tested hardest not in normal markets but in stress — prices falling fast, sector narratives reversing, the conventional wisdom behind your thesis suddenly looking obviously wrong.

In those moments, the only thing standing between you and panic-selling — or the equally destructive doubling down on a deteriorating position — is a deep, specific understanding of what you own and why. Not a bull-case summary. Not a list of smart people who agree with you. A genuine understanding: where the competitive advantage comes from, what could break it, what earnings look like under pessimistic assumptions, what the balance sheet can absorb.

You can't synthesize that from a three-paragraph note. It takes reading earnings transcripts across multiple cycles, understanding the supply chain relationships behind costs and pricing, tracking capital allocation decisions as they compound or destroy value, and judging management on observed behavior rather than reputation.

That's why Principle 1 comes first. If you know where a business sits in its industry structure — what it needs, what it provides, what the alternatives are — you can tell the difference between "this threatens the thesis" and "this is a bad quarter in an intact business."

Conviction isn't stubbornness. Conviction is knowing *why* you own something clearly enough to weigh new information against your thesis instead of against the price. In practice, that distinction is most of what separates durable performance from noise.

---

## A Note on the Framework as a Whole

These principles don't guarantee returns. Nothing does. They're designed to remove the failure modes that cost me the most: investing in things I didn't fully understand, entering positions priced for perfection, buying out of a desire to participate rather than from analysis, and selling good positions at bad times because I mistook price weakness for thesis damage.

The framework is deliberately slow. It misses things. There are stocks I didn't own that went on spectacular runs — businesses I understood well enough to recognize but not well enough to own with conviction, or knew at prices I wouldn't pay. I've made peace with those misses, because the alternative — lowering my standards to play more situations — has a well-documented cost, and it shows up in drawdowns, not in missed gains.

The market rewards patience and punishes urgency, just not always on schedule. The investor who waits until she understands before buying, sizes positions to survive being early and wrong, buys quality at distressed prices, and holds through temporary weakness without confusing it for permanent impairment will, over a full cycle, beat the investor who moves faster on less understanding.

That, at minimum, is what my experience taught me.

---

*Understanding is the precondition for conviction. Conviction is the precondition for patience. Patience is where the returns actually come from.*
