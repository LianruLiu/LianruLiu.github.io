People usually talk about oil and the dollar as two separate things. They aren't. Oil is one of the dollar's instruments — perhaps the most important one. As long as oil holds its strategic position in the world, the dollar stays important. Everything else is commentary.

---

## The Depreciation Is the Strategy

Look at the dollar against the yuan and the euro: it has been sliding in a depreciation channel. That is not an accident. It is the Trump administration's strategy to expand American exports — a cheaper dollar sells more American goods abroad.

But don't confuse the tactic with the trend. Over the long run, the dollar returns to an appreciation channel. Bond yields fall over the long run, and bonds appreciate. The short-term weakness is a tool; the long-term strength is the objective.

And whatever method Washington uses to sustain dollar hegemony, oil is the key instrument.

---

## Sanction Russia, Sell to Europe

The sanctions on Russia during the Ukraine war were, at one level, about reducing Russian oil exports. Consider the geography: Russia is obviously closer to the EU, and buying Russian oil is cheaper and easier for Europe. Sanction Russia, and Europe has to buy its oil from somewhere else — from the United States.

That is the context for Nord Stream. In September 2022, explosions damaged both lines of Nord Stream 1 and one line of Nord Stream 2 near the Danish island of Bornholm. Sweden and Denmark closed their investigations in 2024 without naming a perpetrator; Germany's remains open — two Ukrainian suspects have been arrested on German warrants, one of them charged with orchestrating the blasts on behalf of Ukrainian state entities (a charge Kyiv denies), with a Hamburg trial scheduled for October 2026. No official finding has linked the sabotage to the United States. But if the pipeline is gone, **the direct beneficiary is obvious: America.**

---

## Europe Pays Twice

The EU, by joining America's sanctions on Russia, ended up paying the larger bill. It funds Ukraine with aid money. It spends enormous sums supporting the war. And it pays far more for natural gas than it used to — Eurostat data shows EU oil import values surging more than 55% year-on-year in the second quarter of 2026, while physical volumes barely moved. Europe is spending much more without receiving more energy.

Looked at as long-term planning, the EU is a failed strategist. Its economy faces prolonged stagnation, and on top of that it bears the mounting costs of the Russia-Ukraine war. Twenty-plus countries, each with its own democratic demands, have ground governing efficiency to a halt — and Brussels plainly has no better answer.

If this is all an American encirclement strategy, then the strangulation of Europe has been remarkably successful. It is a siege that looks unsolvable.

---

## Ricardo Would Disapprove

The EU has a strange governing philosophy: it must cater to American strategy while juggling the demands of its own member states.

Take it purely from economics — David Ricardo's theory of trade. Open foreign trade obviously brings a country higher profits and gains. Yet the EU erects a series of odd trade barriers to block Chinese goods. Yes, China's enormous population and cheap labor make its products cheaper and more competitive. But that does not mean Europe should sanction them and protect its own products. As a long-term strategy, it is not optimal: it shelters lower-quality domestic products and dulls the will to compete.

Look at Europe's top companies. They are usually not tech companies — they are consumer companies. Market monopolists. So they lean toward supporting EU barriers against cheaper foreign products, to limit their own losses.

If America is the rule-maker of this competition, and China and the EU are the players, then Europe's strategists are clearly not wise enough. Prolonged inefficiency, bureaucratic drag, and strategic mistakes will cost them decades of sustained economic losses.

---

## The Middleman Play

Now the Trump administration is moving to trade oil with the Putin government. The logic is simple: America buys Russian oil at a cheaper price, which lowers inflation at home — then resells the oil to the world at a markup. To put it bluntly: a middleman's business.

It solves America's inflation problem, while other countries have no choice but to buy their oil from the United States.

The war with Iran follows the same logic — it is an oil strategy. The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world's most important oil export corridors; roughly 20 million barrels a day — about a quarter of the world's seaborne oil trade — pass through it. If Hormuz were shut, the biggest beneficiary would, again, be the United States. The price America pays is reputational: its standing as the guardian of global order takes the hit.

And the attack on Iran was purposeful on Trump's part. His personal belief is that war wins voters and helps the midterms. The strategy deviated — the results were not what he wanted — which is why the messaging keeps shifting. But the objective itself was never in doubt.

---

## My View

Europe's strategists are not wise — on trade or on strategy, they are making long-term mistakes. But here is the uncomfortable part: their strategy is also involuntary. When the rule-maker sets the game, the players don't get to choose whether to play.

Oil is the dollar, and the dollar is enforced through oil. Sanctions redirect flows, pipelines disappear, straits close, and each time the energy map is redrawn, the redrawn map favors Washington. Europe understood the price of defiance and chose the price of compliance instead. It is paying both.

If all of this is truly an American design, then it is an extraordinarily clever one.

---

## Conclusion

As long as oil remains strategically vital, the dollar remains vital — and every tool of American power, from sanctions to sabotage to war, runs through oil. Europe bought into the sanctions regime and received stagnation, expensive gas, and a war bill in return. Its barriers against China protect monopolists, not competitiveness. Its twenty-plus democracies cannot govern efficiently enough to change course.

The siege works because the besieged helped build it. That is what makes it unsolvable — and what makes it brilliant.
