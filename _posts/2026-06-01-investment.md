I did not arrive at my investment framework through theory. I arrived at it through a series of mistakes that were expensive enough to be instructive and early enough to be corrective. The pattern, when I eventually traced it, was consistent: I lost money when I moved before I understood, and I made money when I waited until I did. The framework I use now is built around that observation — extended, stress-tested, and made into a set of principles I try not to violate.

This is not a system for generating alpha by exploiting information asymmetry or running quantitative models. It is a system for avoiding the specific category of errors that destroy long-term returns for most individual investors: acting on incomplete understanding, chasing price momentum, and allowing emotional states — particularly the fear of being left behind — to substitute for analysis.

What follows is that framework, laid out as I actually use it.

---

## Principle 1: The Supply Chain Is the Unit of Analysis — Not the Stock

The single most consistent mistake I made early was treating a company as the unit of analysis. I would research a business — its revenue, its margins, its management team — and build a thesis around whether that business would grow. This is necessary but insufficient. What I was missing was the *system* in which the business operated: who supplied it, who it sold to, what regulated it, what could replace it, and where the actual value in its industry accumulated.

The supply chain is the real unit of analysis. Every industry has a structure — a set of relationships between raw material suppliers, component manufacturers, system integrators, distribution channels, end customers, and regulatory bodies — and within that structure, value does not distribute evenly. It concentrates at the points of scarcity, irreplaceability, or structural leverage. Finding those points is the prior question to asking whether any specific company is cheap or expensive.

**The practical method:** Before investing in any company, I map its industry vertically. Who are the tier-1 and tier-2 suppliers? Where are the gross margins highest in the supply chain, and why? Who has pricing power — the ability to raise prices without losing customers — and what is the source of that power? Is it proprietary technology, regulatory licensing, network effects, switching costs, or simply capital intensity that deters competition?

This exercise frequently produces a counterintuitive result: the most attractive investment in an industry is not the company you initially identified. It is often a supplier two levels upstream, or an enabling infrastructure provider whose customer base is the entire industry rather than a single end market. When I was first forming views on AI infrastructure, my initial instinct was to focus on the model companies. The supply chain analysis pointed to semiconductors, advanced packaging, and power infrastructure — less glamorous, more durable.

The supply chain framework also provides a natural defense against being fooled by growth narratives. A company can have extraordinary revenue growth while being structurally disadvantaged in its supply chain — capturing value creation at the top of the income statement while having it extracted by suppliers or competed away by peers at the margin level. Understanding the supply chain prevents confusing revenue growth with investable value.

## Principle 2: Structure the Portfolio Before Entering Any Position

Most individual investors build portfolios the way they furnish apartments: one piece at a time, as things catch their attention, without a plan for how the pieces relate to each other. The result is a collection of positions with unintentional concentration in certain sectors, unexamined correlations, and no clear logic for how the overall portfolio behaves in different market environments.

I made this mistake for longer than I should have. I had conviction on individual names, but I had not asked the prior question: what kind of portfolio am I trying to build, and how does each new position contribute to or detract from that structure?

**The framework I now use before adding any position:**

*What is the thesis category?* I distinguish between three types of positions. The first is infrastructure bets — long-duration, early-stage holdings in structural transitions that will take five to ten years to fully play out (quantum computing, satellite internet, new energy architecture). These positions are sized to survive being early and wrong for several years. The second is quality compounders — businesses with durable competitive advantages that I expect to compound returns through earnings growth over a full market cycle. The third is cyclical opportunities — shorter-duration positions in quality businesses that have become temporarily mispriced due to sector rotation, earnings disappointment, or macro headwinds. Each category has a different expected holding period, a different position sizing logic, and a different definition of what "working" looks like.

*How does this position interact with existing holdings?* I explicitly check for sector concentration, factor concentration (e.g., adding another high-multiple growth stock when the portfolio already has significant duration risk), and thematic concentration (e.g., adding a second semiconductor position when the thesis is similar to one I already hold). Correlation is not always visible from sector labels — two companies in different industries can have highly correlated earnings if both are exposed to the same underlying economic driver.

*What is the sizing logic?* Position size should reflect conviction *and* the cost of being wrong. A position with a binary outcome — it works spectacularly or loses most of its value — should be sized differently than a position in a stable business where the downside is a few years of underperformance. I use a simple rule: I should never be in a position where a single holding going to zero changes my financial life in a material way. Beyond that floor, sizing scales with conviction and the quality of my informational edge.

*What is the exit framework?* Before entering, I decide under what circumstances I would sell. This prevents the common failure mode of being "married" to a position and rationalizing continued holding as the original thesis deteriorates. There are three legitimate exit conditions: the thesis is wrong (something I believed about the business or industry turns out to be false), the price has reached fair value or beyond (the opportunity has been realized), or a better opportunity has emerged and capital reallocation is warranted. Price decline alone is not a reason to sell a position whose thesis is intact — and this distinction is one of the most practically important ones in portfolio management.

## Principle 3: Do Not Confuse FOMO with a Thesis

Fear of missing out is the most expensive emotional state in investing. It is also the most disguised: FOMO almost always arrives dressed as a thesis. You will find yourself articulating reasons why a stock is worth buying — the market is large, the management team is exceptional, the technology is transformative — while the actual driver of your decision is that the stock has already risen substantially and you do not want to be the person who missed it.

I have made this mistake enough times to recognize its signature: the reasoning comes *after* the emotional pull, not before. When I notice that I am building a case for a position that I first encountered because it was going up, I treat that as a warning signal.

**The diagnostic questions I use:**

*Would I be interested in this company if the stock had been flat for three years?* Many great businesses are genuinely attractive investments after three years of flat or declining stock performance and a period of investor disinterest. Many FOMO-driven purchases involve businesses that have attracted attention primarily because their recent price performance has been strong. If my interest is contingent on recent price momentum, the investment case is weaker than it appears.

*What has changed in the business that was not known or valued twelve months ago?* Prices move ahead of fundamentals in some cases, and prices lag fundamentals in others. If a stock has risen 80% in twelve months, I want to understand specifically what has changed in the underlying business — not the narrative, but the actual economic reality — that justifies that rerating. If the honest answer is "not that much," the price change is more likely sentiment-driven than fundamentals-driven, and the risk/reward for a new entry is materially worse than it appears.

*Am I comfortable with the price if it returns to where it was six months ago?* If the answer is no — if a 30-40% pullback to recent levels would meaningfully impair my thesis or my emotional relationship with the position — then I am not buying value, I am buying momentum. Momentum strategies can work, but they require different discipline and different exit frameworks than value-based investing. Mixing them unconsciously is one of the most reliable ways to experience both the downside of momentum (sharp reversals) and the downside of value (long waiting periods) without the upside of either.

The practical implication: I have a standing bias *against* initiating positions in securities that have had strong recent performance, and a standing bias *toward* securities in sectors that have been out of favor. This does not mean reflexively buying weakness — a declining stock is often declining for good reasons — but it means my default entry conditions favor patience over urgency.

## Principle 4: Growth Phases Demand Caution; Drawdowns Demand Preparation

This is the principle that most directly captures my personal investment temperament, and it is the one I have found most at odds with conventional investment culture, which tends to celebrate participation in strong markets and treat caution as timidity.

My experience has been the opposite. The times I have generated the most durable returns have been when I entered positions during periods of price weakness, investor disinterest, or sector-specific pessimism in businesses with intact or improving fundamentals. The times I have generated the worst outcomes have been when I entered positions that were already well-known, widely owned, and priced for continued strong performance.

**During growth phases and strong markets:**

I do not stop investing during bull markets, but I raise my standards significantly. The question I ask is not "will this business continue to grow?" — many businesses will — but "at what price is that growth already reflected in the valuation, and what incremental positive surprise is required to generate a satisfactory return from here?"

In the later stages of a market cycle, valuations tend to expand as confidence grows, making it increasingly difficult to find situations where the market has underestimated quality or durability. My response is to concentrate existing positions, raise cash levels modestly, and avoid initiating new positions where the entry valuation requires significant multiple expansion to generate acceptable returns. I am willing to underperform in the final innings of a bull market in exchange for lower drawdown risk and better entry points in the subsequent cycle.

This is psychologically difficult. Underperforming in a rising market feels like being wrong even when the underlying logic is sound. The temptation to participate — to add the position "just for now" — is constant. I have learned to treat that temptation as a signal rather than as guidance.

**During drawdowns and periods of sector-specific stress:**

Drawdowns are when I do my most active fundamental work. The specific opportunity that interests me most is the high-quality business in a temporarily impaired sector — a company with durable competitive advantages, strong balance sheet, and intact long-term thesis that is trading at a discount because its sector has fallen out of favor, a macro headwind has suppressed near-term earnings, or sentiment has generalized from legitimate concerns about weaker competitors to the sector as a whole.

The entry conditions I look for: the business has demonstrated its competitive position through a previous cycle, the balance sheet is strong enough to survive a prolonged downturn without dilutive capital raises, the management team has been clear and consistent in its communication (poor earnings communication during difficulty is a signal), and the valuation reflects pessimism rather than realistic earnings power.

I am willing to enter a position that continues to fall after I buy it, provided the thesis is intact. The distinction between "the price went down" and "I was wrong" is one of the most important ones in investing, and confusing them leads to two equal and opposite errors: selling good businesses during temporary drawdowns, and holding bad businesses as their fundamentals deteriorate.

**The asymmetry I am trying to exploit:**

Most investors are psychologically constituted to do the opposite of what this principle recommends — they are more comfortable buying strength than weakness, and more comfortable selling during drawdowns than during rallies. This behavioral asymmetry is well-documented and creates a persistent source of mispricing that patient, disciplined investors can exploit without any informational advantage. The edge is not analytical; it is temperamental.

## Principle 5: The Cost of Premature Entry Is Underappreciated

There is a specific form of impatience that is particularly expensive and particularly common among investors who have done serious research: entering a position before the business has reached the stage where its thesis is ready to be validated by the market.

I have done this repeatedly with early-stage technology infrastructure — entering a position based on a correctly identified structural transition, but too early in the technology maturation curve for the thesis to translate into earnings, and then watching the position decay for two or three years before anything happened. The thesis was right. The timing was wrong. And the psychological cost of holding a position that is losing value while the rest of the market rises is significant — it creates pressure to exit precisely when the thesis is closest to inflection.

**The questions I now ask before entering an early-stage position:**

*What is the catalyst that will move this from "thesis" to "evidence"?* A thesis is a belief about the future. Evidence is what the market will respond to. Identifying the specific milestones — product launches, customer wins, regulatory approvals, earnings inflections — that will cause the market to begin pricing the thesis creates a more disciplined entry framework than simply deciding the long-term story is compelling.

*How long can I hold this without validation, and how am I sizing accordingly?* Early-stage infrastructure positions should be sized to survive a three-to-five year wait without the position having a material psychological impact on the rest of the portfolio. If a position is too large to hold comfortably through a multi-year period of no-feedback, it will be exited at the worst possible time.

*Is there an existing business generating cash flow that I can own while waiting for the early-stage thesis to develop?* Sometimes the same structural transition that creates early-stage opportunity also creates nearer-term opportunity in established businesses that will benefit as enabling infrastructure. Owning the established beneficiary provides portfolio stability and return while the early-stage position develops — and it funds the patience that early-stage investing requires.

## Principle 6: Know What You Own Well Enough to Defend It Under Pressure

The final principle is the one that ties all the others together. Every investment framework is tested most severely not during normal markets but during periods of market stress — when prices fall rapidly, when sector narratives reverse, when the conventional wisdom that supported your thesis suddenly appears obviously wrong.

During those moments, the only thing that prevents panic-selling or, equally destructive, doubling down on deteriorating positions is a deep, clear, specific understanding of what you own and why. Not a summary of the bull case. Not a list of reasons why smart people agree with you. A genuine understanding of the business: where its competitive advantage comes from, what would threaten it, what the realistic earnings trajectory is under pessimistic assumptions, and what the balance sheet can absorb.

This kind of understanding cannot be synthesized from a three-paragraph investment note. It requires reading earnings transcripts across multiple cycles, understanding the supply chain relationships that drive the business's costs and pricing, tracking the capital allocation decisions that compound or destroy value over time, and having a view on management quality that is based on observed behavior rather than reputation.

The supply chain analysis in Principle 1 is the foundation for this. If you understand where a business sits in its industry structure — what it needs, what it provides, and what alternatives exist — you will be far better positioned to distinguish between "this is a problem that threatens the thesis" and "this is a difficult quarter in a business that remains fundamentally intact."

Conviction is not the same as stubbornness. Conviction is knowing *why* you own something clearly enough that you can evaluate new information against your thesis rather than against the current price. That distinction, in practice, is most of what separates durable investment performance from the noise.

## A Note on the Framework as a Whole

These principles do not guarantee returns. No framework does. What they are designed to do is remove the specific failure modes that have cost me the most: investing in things I did not fully understand, entering positions that were already priced for perfection, being driven by the desire to participate rather than by genuine analysis, and exiting good positions at bad times because I confused price weakness with thesis deterioration.

The framework is deliberately slow. It misses things. There are stocks I have not owned that performed spectacularly — businesses I understood well enough to recognize but not well enough to own with conviction, or businesses I knew at prices I was not willing to pay. I have made peace with those misses, because the alternative — lowering my standards to participate in more situations — has a well-documented cost that shows up in drawdowns rather than in missed gains.

The market rewards patience and punishes urgency in ways that are not always immediate. The investor who waits until she understands before buying, sizes positions to survive being early and wrong, buys quality at distressed prices, and holds through temporary weakness without confusing it for permanent impairment will, over a full cycle, do better than the investor who acts faster on less understanding.

That is, at minimum, what my experience has taught me.

*Understanding is the precondition for conviction. Conviction is the precondition for patience. Patience is where the returns actually come from.*
